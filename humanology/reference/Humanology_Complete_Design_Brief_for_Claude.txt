# Part One The Humanology App

## 1 Product purpose

Humanology is a science-backed personal health improvement app that helps adults discover useful small habits, understand the evidence behind them, choose the habits that fit their lives, and practice them consistently. It combines a simple daily plan with a deeper, browsable scientific habit library. Optional rewards add a tangible acknowledgement of participation.

The core promise is: A healthier you. Backed by science.

Supporting explanation: Discover research-backed habits, understand why they may help, and build a routine that fits your life.

Humanology should help someone answer five practical questions: What could I change? How credible is the evidence? Why might this fit my circumstances? What exactly do I do today? What am I noticing over time?

The original emphasis was cardiovascular health, extending into anxiety and stress support, sleep, fatigue, and broader heart, mind, and body outcomes. Retain that substance while using approachable everyday language. A person can explore blood-pressure-related research without the app presenting itself as a doctor or promising treatment.

Many suggested actions are measurable micro-habits taking less than 15 minutes. This is a starting philosophy, not a universal duration limit. Some meaningful routines require longer sessions or several sessions a week. Show the actual time and frequency instead of compressing every behavior into an implausibly tiny intervention.

The product must work for two kinds of engagement. A user can open Today, do one clear thing, and leave. Another user can browse the library, compare evidence, inspect studies, and build a custom stack. Both should feel like parts of the same app.

## 2 Intended users and emotional experience

Design primarily for adults who want better everyday health but feel overwhelmed by conflicting advice or struggle to turn intentions into routines. They may be busy parents, workers with limited spare time, skeptical readers who want sources, or people who enjoy learning about health without wanting a clinical dashboard every morning.

Do not assume an athlete, a wearable owner, a young single person, or a person whose primary goal is weight loss. Support different starting points, mobility levels, schedules, and comfort with tracking. Begin with one manageable habit by default and a manual check-in.

The intended emotional sequence is curiosity, confidence, a manageable decision, a small accomplishment, and willingness to return. The app should feel like thoughtful help from a warm, intelligent guide. The science should make the user confident; the interface should make participation feel approachable.

Avoid guilt for missing a day. Never imply that poor health reflects poor character. Do not make an illustrated person suffer, become thinner, lose affection, or look disappointed because someone misses a habit. Support normal breaks, illness, travel, caregiving, and changes in routine.

## 3 Core experience

The primary journey is: understand the science-led promise; answer a few questions; receive a manageable plan; inspect the reason and evidence behind each suggestion; schedule the actions; complete or adjust them; review progress; optionally earn redeemable rewards; refine the plan over time.

Daily use should take seconds when a person only wants to record a habit. More detail should be available through deliberate taps. A completion action should never require reading a research paper, and opening the research should never require completing a habit first.

The default starter plan contains one recommended habit. Show exactly what to do, when and for how long, why it fits this user, and what evidence supports trying it. Offer Add another as a secondary choice for people who want more; do not require choosing several habits. Allow swapping the recommendation, choosing a simpler version, or changing its schedule. The full library and multi-habit plans remain available. The plan should reflect what the user already does so it does not present established routines as new opportunities.

The first prototype priority is one complete habit journey: goals, recommendation, concrete science, scheduling, one completion, a useful review, and an adjustment to the next plan. Establish that journey before polishing every secondary screen. This is a sequence for design work, not a deletion of the full product scope.

## 4 Four concepts that must remain distinct

| Concept | Meaning | Suggested presentation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Evidence | Confidence in a defined research claim | Letter grade, readable label, and research link |
| Personal fit | Relevance to stated goals and circumstances | A short explanation of why the habit was suggested |
| Progress | Participation in the user's scheduled routine | Completion counts, milestones, and history |
| Rewards | Redeemable value under specific earning rules | A separate points balance and gift-card progress |

Do not combine these into a single medical-looking health score. A completed checklist does not measure overall health. A highly supported habit does not necessarily offer the greatest additional benefit to someone already doing it. Reward points do not measure scientific quality or effort intensity.

If a numerical evidence score is explored later, define and explain its methodology before presenting numbers. For the initial design, letters plus plain-language explanations preserve the original reliability-score concept without invented precision such as 93.7 out of 100.

## 5 Scientific evidence and editorial model

Use A/B/C as the ordinary library vocabulary. Preserve D/X in the methodology and research-only views so the earlier expanded idea remains represented. The following is a proposed Humanology editorial framework, subject to expert refinement before public use.

| Level | User-facing label | Intended meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Strong evidence | A substantial, directly relevant body of human research supports the stated outcome, with appropriate quality and consistency |
| B | Moderate evidence | Human research supports the stated outcome, with meaningful limitations or remaining uncertainty |
| C | Preliminary evidence | Early, small, or otherwise limited human research that needs cautious interpretation |
| D | Observational evidence | Associations in observational research; doing the habit has not thereby been shown to cause the outcome |
| X | Not established | Insufficient appropriate human evidence to recommend the behavior for the stated outcome |
| Unreviewed | Evidence review pending | An editorial workflow state, not an evidence grade |

The earlier proposal associated A with multiple large human randomized trials and high-quality meta-analyses; B with more limited trials or a strong synthesis; C with pilot studies; D with observational reports; and X with animal, theoretical, or anecdotal evidence. Retain that history, but do not automate grades by counting studies. A meta-analysis can summarize weak evidence. Consider risk of bias, consistency, precision, directness, studied population, dose, and publication limitations.

The grade belongs to a particular behavior and outcome in a particular research context. A habit can have different evidence for sleep, mood, or blood pressure. Design claim-level grades within a habit's detail page. Do not let one strong outcome imply that every proposed benefit is equally supported.

Keep study effects distinct from the starter action. A ten-minute beginner action may be a useful way to begin a longer program, but it must not inherit the numerical effect of a different dose or population without justification. Mechanistic plausibility also does not establish that a behavior produces a meaningful benefit.

A habit's research area should accommodate: a plain-language summary; the outcome measured; research design; participants and population limitations; intervention, frequency, duration, and comparison; findings; verified effect size with units and uncertainty where appropriate; conflicting evidence; cautions; source links; last editorial review; and correction history. Only display a reviewer or institution when that relationship actually exists.

The prototype may use clearly labeled demonstration grades to show visual hierarchy. It must not fabricate study titles, PubMed identifiers, DOI links, participant counts, effect percentages, reviewer names, or publication dates. Use verified sources when supplied; otherwise show Evidence review pending and a clearly labeled source placeholder. Keep the prototype disclaimer visible within science views and in the demonstration guide.

## 6 Habit library and content structure

Organize the library around approachable benefit categories, with optional Heart, Mind, and Body collections as broader entry points. Proposed categories are Movement and Strength; Sleep and Daily Rhythm; Calm and Stress Support; Nutrition and Hydration; Mobility and Posture; Daylight and Environment; and Everyday Well-being. Energy and Fatigue can be a goal filter spanning categories instead of forcing every habit into a single bucket.

Historical topics to preserve in the content inventory include breathing exercises, garlic, posture, light exposure, hydration, and warm water. Recent examples include short walks, consistent sleep routines, resistance exercise, mindfulness, and reading. These are research candidates and demonstration topics, not a list already certified as effective. In particular, do not present garlic or warm water as proven treatments because they appeared in a discussion. Form, dose, population, safety, and the specific claim require review.

Each habit record should support these information groups:

- Identity: title, short action, category, an optional supplied human activity figure or a coded category icon, and unique content identifier.
- Practical action: starter version, full studied version where relevant, steps, duration, frequency, equipment, setting, time of day, effort, accessible alternatives, and optional video.
- Science: outcome-specific grades, plain-language benefit, mechanism, sources, limitations, cautions, review date, and editorial status. Record the studied population, intervention, session duration, frequency, study length, measured outcome, and verified result so the interface can compare the research with the selected starter action.
- Personal relevance: matching goals, current baseline, explanation of fit, reasons to adapt, and alternatives.
- Routine: selected days, time or cue, reminders, stack membership, pause state, and completion rule.
- Tracking: binary completion, count, duration, or other appropriate measure; self-reported or imported source; optional note; and history.
- Rewards: whether an action can qualify under the optional program, with eligibility described separately from its evidence grade.

Make discovery practical from its first view. Offer visible entry points such as By goal, By time, and By ability or adaptation. Useful choices include five minutes or less, no equipment, seated options, low-impact movement, and beginner-friendly actions when those adaptations are supported by the content. Let people describe what they can comfortably do without requiring a diagnosis or disability label.

Offer search by ordinary words such as sleep, energy, walking, or stress. Filters should include category or goal, evidence level, time required, ability or adaptation, effort, equipment, and frequency. Label sorting clearly: strongest evidence, shortest time, or best fit for selected goals. These are different orderings. Include saved habits, recently viewed items, and a beginner collection. Preserve a path for the question: What has strong evidence, fits in ten minutes, and suits what I can comfortably do?

## 7 Navigation and information architecture

Recommended mobile navigation is Today, Discover, Progress, and Rewards. Access Profile and Settings through a persistent account button. My Plan belongs inside Today; Saved Habits and Stacks are reachable from Discover and the plan editor. Scientific methodology belongs in both the relevant evidence sheet and the About section.

When rewards are disabled, show three primary tabs and remove reward promotions throughout the app. Do not leave a nonfunctional fourth tab or a promise of gift cards. When rewards are available but the user hides them, keep a secondary way to reach the balance and existing redemptions.

On wider screens, use a left navigation rail, a constrained main column, and a useful secondary panel where appropriate. A Today screen might pair the plan with a small weekly summary; a research page might pair the article with its evidence facts. The desktop layout must feel designed for the available space.

Maintain stable back behavior, selected tab, scroll position, and filter state. A user opening a habit from search should return to the same results, not the app's home screen.

## 9 Welcome and onboarding in detail

### 9.1 Welcome

Use a white or subtly warm-white canvas with generous breathing room. Put a coded Humanology wordmark and a visually confident painted human figure above or beside the headline, preserving the headline and primary action as readable interface elements. Use A healthier you. Backed by science. as the leading option. Follow with: Small, practical habits. Clear evidence. A routine that fits your life.

Primary action: Build my plan. Secondary action: Explore the science. Existing-member link: Sign in. A compact preview can show a habit with an evidence badge. Rewards, when enabled, appear as a supporting benefit rather than the main purchase proposition.

### 9.2 How it works

Explain choosing one useful starting habit, understanding its research, and building a manageable routine. Mention that additional habits can be added when wanted. Use concise copy and no more than a few illustrated panels. Allow skipping or moving directly into the questionnaire. A separate optional rewards panel can explain earned points without implying income.

### 9.3 Goals

Ask What would you like to work on? Offer Better sleep; More daily movement; Strength and mobility; A calmer routine; More everyday energy; Cardiovascular well-being; and A healthier routine overall. Let users select a main goal and optional additional interests. Avoid a medical diagnosis questionnaire disguised as casual onboarding.

### 9.4 Starting point

Ask which habits are already part of the person's life and what feels difficult to maintain. Use a few friendly choices instead of long mandatory forms. Examples include regular movement, a consistent sleep routine, and taking short breaks. Support I am not sure and Skip. This information changes recommendations rather than merely decorating a profile.

### 9.5 Time and practical preferences

Ask how much time feels manageable: about five minutes, ten minutes, fifteen minutes, or a flexible amount. Ask preferred time of day and whether activities should require no equipment. Include accessibility or seated alternatives without requiring a disability label. Do not prescribe a longer plan than the selected time while claiming it fits.

### 9.6 Connection and privacy choice

Explain that connections may simplify tracking. Offer Set up later with equal prominence to Connect when appropriate. Ask for permissions only when a relevant action is chosen. The prototype must show simulated permissions rather than imitating an authentic operating-system authorization grant.

### 9.7 Recommended plan

Lead with one recommended habit and a clear action to start it. Show the action, duration, frequency, evidence label, and Why this fits you explanation. Provide View the science, Swap, and Adjust controls. A secondary Add another action exposes optional additional recommendations or the library without suggesting that the initial choice is inadequate. Show total commitment only for the habits the user actually selects. Do not display a default three-card commitment screen.

Use transparent matching explanations: You chose a brief movement habit and prefer activities without equipment. Do not imply Humanology predicted the person's medical response.

### 9.8 Scheduling and account

Let the person assign a time, existing routine cue, or flexible slot. Account creation can occur after the value of the plan is visible. Simulate email or social sign-in states without capturing real passwords. The person must be able to inspect the recommendation and its science, schedule the habit, complete one occurrence, and see the result before a blocking subscription offer. No payment card is required to complete this starter experience. This is a free first experience, not an invented auto-renewing trial.

### 9.9 Plan ready

Summarize the one selected starter habit, its next occurrence, and the reminder preference. Show additional habits only if the user deliberately added them. Use a calm visual acknowledgement. Primary action: Go to Today. Preserve progress if the user goes back to edit a question. Returning users should not be forced through onboarding again.

## 10 Today and daily habit interaction

Today is the most important screen. Directly below a compact date and account header, give the next planned action the strongest visual priority. A new user with one habit should see that habit immediately. Keep greeting, navigation, and progress supporting it. Avoid stacking a large illustration, upsell, reward promotion, calendar, and multiple charts above the action.

Suggested order: compact date and profile header; next habit action with duration, evidence badge, and completion control; any remaining planned actions; then a compact schedule or week selector and concise progress summary. Optional check-in, science discovery, and reward progress sit below the routine or behind a deliberate expansion. Do not require all optional modules on a sparse first-day screen.

A habit card contains the action title, an optional painted human activity figure or a coded category icon, duration or target, time or cue, a readable evidence grade, completion status, and one obvious primary action. Give a featured person enough size to read the pose and brushwork; compact list rows can use the coded icon. Use coded icons only or place a decorative art banner above the next action. A week selector is useful only if it preserves this action-first hierarchy. Example action text: Take a short walk. Secondary text: 10 minutes, after lunch. A science link stays visible or one tap away. The completion control must be distinct from the card link so a user does not accidentally mark completion while reading.

A straightforward completion should take one tap from Today, followed by a short confirmation with Undo. A straightforward reschedule should take two taps: open Move, then choose a suitable quick option such as Later today, Tomorrow, or the next planned slot. Keep a full date-and-time editor available for precise changes without putting it in every routine interaction. Rescheduling must preserve a twice-weekly or other chosen frequency rather than adding extra sessions automatically.

Completion should update the card, daily count, history, and any eligible reward activity consistently. An accidental tap followed by undo must not create duplicate points. Guided sessions can open a timer or instruction panel when appropriate. Evidence remains visible on the card and one tap away in more detail.

States to design: due now; later today; complete; skipped; rescheduled; paused; imported completion; waiting for sync; locally saved; and save failed. Skip today, pause the habit, and remove from plan are different actions. Show the consequences clearly.

If a habit is scheduled twice weekly, the other days are not failures. A day with no scheduled actions should read Nothing scheduled today or Your plan has a rest day. An all-complete day should let the person leave with satisfaction. A missed day should offer a smaller next step or a restart, without a red failure screen.

Past-day corrections are allowed for tracking, with the date clearly shown. The optional reward program can limit retroactive earning separately. Time-zone changes must not duplicate a day or reset a streak unpredictably.

## 11 Habit detail and research screens

The habit detail is the second signature screen. Use a welcoming illustration, title, concise practical action, and prominent evidence label. Present the content in sections or tabs such as Overview, How to do it, and The science. On a long mobile page, a sticky Add to my plan or Edit my plan action can remain available without covering content.

Overview explains the intended outcome, expected effort, frequency, equipment, starter version, and why the habit might fit. Where the user has chosen a relevant goal, show the matching explanation. If the user already practices the habit, offer tracking or refinement rather than claiming a new benefit.

How to do it gives clear ordered steps, a reasonable starter version, and adaptations. Offer optional audio or instructional video only when content exists. Otherwise use a clearly identified placeholder in the prototype. Include captions or a transcript and controls for pause, volume, and playback. Avoid autoplay.

The science begins with a concrete comparison: What researchers tested and Your starting action. Show the studied population, action, frequency, duration, and outcome when verified, alongside the user's practical starter action. Add a brief What this means for you explanation of any difference in dose, population, or timeframe. If the starter action is only an adoption step toward the studied routine, say so plainly. Keep this comparison near the top of The science, rather than burying it in the source list.

Then provide claim-specific evidence badges, what remains uncertain, mechanism information, and verified sources. Offer progressively deeper detail rather than an uninterrupted paper-like wall of text. Do not invent a comparison when source details are missing; show the review state clearly.

A research source card includes verified title, author or organization, year, publication, study type, studied population, and a source link. An effect-size section needs units, time period, comparison, and uncertainty. A source list with no accessible full text should still display the bibliographic information and available abstract link.

Show Why this grade? beside the grade. The explanation must match the actual claim and editorial review. Mechanism is a separate labeled section. A plausible biological story must not visually masquerade as proof.

Include Last reviewed, Content version, and Report a source issue when real values are available. If the evidence changes, the interface should support an update note without silently rewriting a user's prior saved explanation. A removed or revised habit needs a readable transition state.

## 12 Evidence methodology

Create a friendly About our evidence screen. Explain the letters in ordinary language, how sources are selected, why human studies matter for human recommendations, what limitations mean, and why strong evidence is not a personal guarantee. Retain the D and X explanations in an expanded research section.

Explain that Humanology's grades are editorial assessments unless a validated external system is formally adopted. Do not claim government approval or certification. The no-heart logo choice is a design constraint from earlier discussions, not a statement that heart symbols are universally prohibited by law.

The research-only collection can discuss popular ideas with insufficient evidence. These items should not appear as recommended proven actions or earn a high grade merely because they are popular. Give users a useful explanation of uncertainty without ridiculing their questions.

## 13 My Plan and habit stacks

My Plan shows active habits, their schedule, time commitment, reminders, and pause state. Provide Edit, Reorder, Add a habit, and View paused habits. Show a manageable total rather than encouraging users to collect as many habits as possible.

The schedule editor supports selected days, times or broad time windows, an existing cue such as after lunch, target duration or quantity where appropriate, start date, and optional reminder. Changes apply prospectively by default so historical completion percentages remain interpretable.

Habit stacks group actions around a familiar cue: After breakfast, During a work break, or Before bed. Each action retains its own evidence grade and completion history. A stack can have a total duration and a sequence, but it must not inherit a scientific grade as though the combination itself were studied.

Create stack list, stack detail, and create/edit stack views. Support adding, removing, and reordering steps; a keyboard-accessible alternative to dragging; pausing the whole stack; and marking individual actions. Completing a stack must not automatically claim every action occurred unless the user confirms it.

Custom personal habits may be useful for routine management, but they should be labeled Personal habit with no scientific grade. Their reward eligibility is a separate decision; do not award arbitrary custom habits as verified science-backed actions by default.

## 14 Guided sessions

Create a reusable guided-session layout for a breathing practice, short movement routine, or timer. Include the title, instructions, readable timer, Start, Pause, Resume, Finish, and an unobtrusive exit. Show whether leaving saves partial progress. Keep completion rules explicit.

A slow expanding shape can support a breathing session if the content is reviewed, but reduced-motion users need a static or text alternative. Do not imply that an attractive timer makes an unreviewed intervention clinically effective. Avoid forced breath holds or escalating intensity in demonstration content.

A supplied painted human figure may demonstrate a relaxed pose or accompany a completion, using the same style and cast as the rest of the app. The timer, breathing shape, instructions, controls, and feedback are coded. The person must not obscure instructions or act as an unqualified clinician. Keep sound optional and off until requested.

## 15 Check-ins and personal logs

Preserve the original mood, symptom, energy, fatigue, and sleep-quality logging idea. Offer a quick optional check-in that can be completed in a few taps. Suggested fields are mood, energy, perceived stress, sleep quality, and an optional note. Let people select which metrics they want to track.

Use consistent labeled scales with accessible text, such as Very low through Very high, and a clear numerical mapping if charts need numbers. Explain the direction of each scale. High stress and high energy do not mean the same thing. Do not label an informal mood question as a validated anxiety assessment.

For the optional mood flow, use one expressive painted portrait and five clear text choices, for example Low, A bit low, Okay, Good, and Great. Selecting a choice previews the corresponding supplied image when available (otherwise the selected text remains visible); Save check-in records the value. Cancel or Skip leaves history unchanged. Keep one person, crop, wardrobe, and lighting across every expression in that sequence. Let gender and ethnicity vary across the wider app, not between the five moods of one sequence. Labels and selected-state controls are coded and remain understandable without the portrait.

The expression follows only the mood explicitly selected or logged by the user. Do not infer a mood from habits, wearables, inactivity, subscription status, or reward points. Completing an action must not automatically turn a low mood into a happy face. Mood choice does not change earning eligibility or the amount of a reward. A low-mood entry can optionally lead to a user-controlled smaller-plan suggestion; it must not silently change the plan. Do not portray a racial or gender group as the default negative state.

Provide edit, delete, and history views. A missing entry stays missing. Notes should never become public or appear in reward-provider data. Sensitive check-ins should be skippable and should not determine whether a person deserves a reward.

For symptom tracking, keep the first version user-selected and descriptive. Do not create an unreviewed diagnostic engine, symptom severity triage, or medication adjustment flow. If specialized medical interpretation is added later, it needs its own product and content specification.

## 16 Measurements and device connections

Preserve the original data interests: blood pressure, resting heart rate, heart-rate variability, sleep, and activity. Include optional manual readings from home devices, with date, time, units, source, and an optional context note. A blood-pressure record has systolic and diastolic values and may include pulse. Display measurements as logged data, not a diagnosis.

The earlier integration ideas included Apple Watch, Fitbit, Garmin, and Bluetooth home blood-pressure monitors. Apple Health or HealthKit and Android Health Connect are possible connection routes discussed more recently. Show them as planned or simulated integrations until actual support is verified. Do not imply that a web prototype can already read native health data or connect to any brand of cuff.

The Connections screen lists the source, supported data categories, permissions, last sync, and connection state. Include not connected, connecting, connected, partial permission, denied, disconnected, stale, error, and reconnect states. Provide a clear explanation before requesting a permission and a manual alternative afterward.

Imported records should show where they came from. If two devices supply the same activity, duplicate records must not inflate habit completion or points. HRV and sleep metrics from different devices may use different methods; do not merge unlike measures into an unexplained single trend.

Disconnecting a device should explain the difference between stopping future imports and deleting existing records. Use realistic sync timestamps in the demo, always marked as sample data. Do not display a live or connected label unless the prototype explicitly says it is simulated.

## 17 Progress and reflection

The Progress overview should first answer What did I do this week? and What would make next week work better? Lead with completed scheduled actions, the current routine, and one clear route to review or adjust the plan. Then offer selected logs, heatmaps, measurement trends, and the exploratory What am I noticing? views. Do not overwhelm a new user with empty charts or make chart interpretation necessary for a useful review.

Provide week, month, and longer-range views where meaningful. Display denominators for percentages. An example of 9 out of 12 scheduled actions is 75 percent completion; it is not a 75 percent health score. Scheduled rest days, paused periods, and skipped actions need distinct treatment in the legend.

For adherence percentages, count scheduled actions whose due window has ended in the selected period. Exclude future actions and prospectively scheduled rest or paused periods. An action that was due and then skipped remains in that historical denominator. Preserve the schedule that applied at the time so deleting a habit today does not rewrite last month's adherence. Today's planning summary may show all actions scheduled today, but label that as a plan count rather than a completed-period adherence rate.

Preserve the calendar heatmap. Let users switch between completion and a selected metric, but change the legend and units clearly. Missing data needs a neutral pattern or empty state separate from a genuine zero or a low value. Clicking a day opens the underlying entries.

A habit history page shows schedule, completed actions, edits, and notes. A metric trend page shows units, date range, source, individual values, gaps, and a text summary. Keep charts legible and avoid dual axes that visually exaggerate a relationship.

The weekly review should show a concise factual recap, ask whether the plan was manageable, and let the user make a practical decision about next week. Offer Keep this plan, Make it easier, Change the schedule, or Swap the habit. Show a simple preview of next week's action, frequency, and estimated commitment before applying a change. Preserve the user's history and show how a rewards-enabled schedule change affects future earning; do not silently rewrite either.

Suggested summaries can say You completed three of four planned walks. They must not say that an unverified health change was caused by those walks. Include this review and next-week adjustment in the first complete prototype journey, using clearly labeled sample history where time has not actually elapsed.

## 18 Exploratory habit and outcome comparisons

Preserve the original habit-to-biometric correlation concept as an advanced exploration view. Users can compare a behavior with a chosen recorded metric over the same period. Show dates, number of usable observations, missing data, and sources. Provide an insufficient-data state.

Use language such as These patterns appeared together in your records. Explain that an association does not establish cause and can reflect other changes, measurement differences, or chance. Do not manufacture percentages of improvement, statistical confidence, or claims that one behavior lowered blood pressure from a mock chart.

A more sophisticated analysis belongs later. For the first prototype, a coherent demonstration of two aligned timelines and a cautious narrative is enough. The editorial and analytical rules for real automated insights must be designed separately before launch.

## 19 Optional rewards program

Rewards are an optional product module, not an approved launch commitment. The founder wants everyone to have a defined way to earn tangible value through participation, potentially redeemable for an Amazon-style gift card. Preserve that intention. The model is earned value with published rules; random winners, wagers, prize pools, and competitive leaderboards are outside the selected direction.

Build the full rewards experience as interactive HTML/CSS/JavaScript so it can be enabled or disabled without changing the core app. Every balance, progress bar, gift-card representation, ledger, earning explanation, confirmation, and redemption state is coded; no raster reward artwork or screenshot-based rewards screen is allowed. When disabled, remove reward claims from onboarding, subscription copy, completion messages, navigation, and profile. When enabled, the habit experience must remain useful after a user reaches the reward cap.

The revised design must be fair across different appropriate schedules. A person following a twice-weekly routine should have the same possible reward ceiling as a person following a daily routine. More scheduled days, more intense exercise, or a higher evidence grade should not automatically mean more money. This replaces the earlier proposed award of 50 points per participation day, which disadvantaged people with fewer scheduled days.

### 19.1 Proposed rules for a coherent prototype

The following is one suggested accounting model to make the prototype concrete. Its exact rules are a design proposal, not a finalized business policy. Keep it replaceable if the founder chooses another equally fair model.

Consumer explanation: Follow the routine you chose. Its scheduled sessions share up to 1,000 points each paid period. A twice-weekly routine and a daily routine can earn the same total.

| Item | Demonstration rule |
| --- | --- |
| Subscription | Proposed price of $6.99 per month |
| Earning focus | One eligible focus routine chosen from content-supported habits; the initial routine normally contains the one starter habit |
| Earning event | Completing an eligible scheduled session once |
| Session allocation | The period's 1,000-point budget is divided across the actual eligible sessions in the selected schedule, with whole-point rounding |
| Billing-period maximum | 1,000 points per paid subscription period across the account, regardless of habit frequency |
| Conversion | 1,000 points equals $1 of gift-card value |
| Example redemption | 5,000 points for a $5 gift card |
| Carryover | Earned points remain in the balance between periods |
| Reset | A new earning allocation begins with the next paid billing period; the earned balance does not reset |
| Extra habits | Adding more habits does not create another reward budget |
| Transfer or purchase | No buying, selling, or transferring points in this concept |
| Cash withdrawal | Not included in the proposed first version |
| Extra intensity | No additional money for longer, harder, or riskier activity |

Show the actual paid period dates and count the sessions scheduled within those dates. Do not assume every billing period is four weeks or thirty days. A valid twice-weekly schedule with eight sessions in the sample period allocates 125 points to each. A ten-session schedule allocates 100 each. A thirty-session schedule allocates 33 points to twenty sessions and 34 points to ten sessions. Completing the full eligible schedule earns exactly 1,000 points in every example.

Assign each scheduled session a stable whole-point allocation when the plan is established, distributing any remainder deterministically across the schedule. The allocations must sum to exactly 1,000. Show the next session's value in its reward explanation. The value reflects the schedule allocation, not how healthy, hard, or scientifically valuable the activity is. Do not calculate a second, conflicting cumulative formula elsewhere in the interface.

The focus routine must use a suitable frequency supported by the content and any offered adaptation. Do not invite users to invent a one-session monthly plan simply to collect the full allowance. The eventual eligibility policy needs to define acceptable schedules, adaptations, enrollment timing, and recording windows. In the prototype, show a clear supported choice rather than an unrestricted reward-schedule loophole.

A missed session's allocation remains unearned and is not automatically redistributed. Rest days with no scheduled session are not misses. On a prospective plan change, preserve completed, pending, and past missed allocations; redistribute only the still-future unused allocation across the revised eligible future schedule. Preview the change before confirming it. A simple move of the same occurrence keeps its identity and allocation. Moving an occurrence across a billing boundary must not create a second award; the final cross-period policy remains a prelaunch decision.

For example, a ten-session plan starts with 100 points per session. After two completed sessions, one missed session, and seven future sessions, the account has earned 200 points; the missed 100 remains unearned; and 700 is still allocated to future sessions. If a supported prospective plan change leaves two future sessions, those sessions can share the remaining 700. Completing both brings this period's earned amount to 900, not 1,000. The past miss is preserved. Pausing itself earns nothing; if no eligible sessions remain, the unused amount stays unearned. Do not offer plan changes as a way to erase misses or accelerate rewards.

Pending awards reserve their session allocations against the same billing-period maximum. A session can be credited only once, whether recorded manually, imported, rescheduled, or corrected. Use the account's chosen time zone to display dates, but changing that zone or importing the same activity later must not create a new earning event.

Under this proposed paid-member model, the free first-habit experience records progress without issuing redeemable points. Demonstrate real-value reward behavior with a separate paid-member sample profile. Do not show free users an earned balance that later disappears or requires payment to unlock. Any introductory reward would need separate published rules and funding.

Reward participation in a reasonable action, not a lower weight, lower blood pressure, longer fast, larger water intake, or better mood rating. Optional device connections can simplify recording, but provide a comparable manual path. Do not require access to sensitive health data or enablement of system functions as the price of obtaining rewards. See the platform notes in Appendix D.

For motivation, use completion counts, personal milestones, and optional streaks. The earlier idea of unlimited nonredeemable motivational points can remain a future variant. The simpler default reserves the word points for the redeemable balance. If both systems are shown, label them distinctly and never imply progress points can be exchanged for gift cards.

### 19.2 Honest economics in the design

At 1,000 points per paid period, a new member starting at zero needs five fully earned periods to reach a 5,000-point reward. Do not imply a $5 gift card will arrive within a few days. With 10,000 paid subscribers and a $1 face-value allowance each, the maximum new face-value liability is $10,000 per paid period, before applicable fees. These are arithmetic examples of the proposed budget, not forecasts of uptake, retention, profitability, or redemption cost.

Unredeemed balances remain outstanding obligations in the business model. Expiration, refunds, fees, and cancelled subscriptions need final program decisions. A $1 reward allocation does not by itself establish profit after billing fees, taxes, operating costs, and acquisition spending.

The earlier suggestion of a $5 welcome reward after three paid months is not included by default: the ordinary allowance over those periods is only $3. A larger welcome bonus would need a separately funded acquisition promotion. Merchant-funded discounts, free products, or sponsored offers are later options, but do not assume a sponsor is already available or that a discount has the same value as a gift card.

### 19.3 Rewards home

Show available points, their gift-card equivalent, progress to a selected reward, this period's confirmed and pending earnings, the period maximum, and the next reset date. Include the selected focus routine, its schedule, the allocation for its next eligible session, a concise How to earn explanation, and an activity ledger. Keep the ordinary consumer explanation short; put calculation details in an expandable explanation.

Label the remaining opportunity precisely as Future scheduled points. It is the sum of unearned allocations still attached to future eligible sessions, not always 1,000 minus confirmed earnings. Missed sessions and pending reservations can make those numbers different. Separate an available balance from pending points and a maximum from an earned amount. Do not describe a missed allocation as a debt or fine.

Use this consistent primary example: a paid member has 3,200 points available, equal to $3.20 in gift-card value; a 5,000-point reward is 64 percent funded, with 1,800 points remaining. The selected routine has ten scheduled sessions in the sample paid period, each allocated 100 points. The ledger contains 3,000 carried from earlier periods and 200 confirmed from two completed sessions this period. With no misses, eight future sessions hold 800 future scheduled points.

When the third session is completed, show 3,200 available, 100 pending, and 700 still attached to future sessions. After the brief simulated confirmation, show 3,300 available, 300 confirmed this period, 66 percent progress to the gift card, 1,700 points remaining, and 700 future scheduled points. Pending awards count against the session and period allocation, preventing duplicates. Update every view together.

The reward card can say $5 gift card and use Amazon as a clearly labeled merchant example. The actual merchant, country, denomination, and provider are not confirmed. Do not display an official partnership seal or a fabricated merchant-approved graphic.

### 19.4 Gift-card detail and redemption

The gift-card detail shows merchant, currency, region, denomination, point cost, terms, availability, and delivery method. Insufficient balance should explain the remaining amount without an aggressive subscription upsell. A progress estimate must reflect eligible scheduled allocations, missed sessions, and the remaining period ceiling. Display an estimate as conditional on future completion, never as a guaranteed payout date.

At a sufficient demo balance, open a redemption-review screen. Show the point cost, resulting balance, delivery destination, and relevant restrictions. Confirm before simulating issuance. Prevent double taps from creating duplicate orders.

While processing, reserve the points and show Pending. A successful mock redemption shows a completed transaction and a visibly fictitious delivery preview, not a realistic usable code. A confirmed failure releases the reserved points. A temporary delay keeps a single pending order rather than inviting repeated purchases.

Provide ledger entries for earned, pending, available, redeemed, reversed, and failed transactions, with date and plain-language reasons. A reversal must reference the original event. Avoid mysterious deductions.

### 19.5 Edge cases

Design states for zero balance; period maximum reached; This scheduled session is already credited; completion undone; a prospective schedule change; missed session; paused routine; provider unavailable; merchant unavailable; unsupported region; delivery address unverified; pending payment; cancellation scheduled; subscription expired; and disputed transaction.

When a completion is undone before confirmation, remove that session's pending award. If the award was confirmed but remains unspent, reverse its exact allocation from the available balance and the corresponding period's confirmed total. In the primary ten-session example, undoing the third completion reverses 100 points and returns the balance from 3,300 to 3,200. Preserve a reversal entry and the session's original identity and allocation. Re-recording that same eligible occurrence can restore that allocation within the program's recording window; it must not mint a new one or transfer the award to an unrelated event. Corrections after redemption need a defined support policy; the prototype should not create an unexplained negative balance.

The proposed cancellation design stops new earning after the paid-through date and preserves visible earned balances. Treatment of a balance below the minimum after cancellation remains an explicit prelaunch policy decision. Do not silently erase it or invent a guarantee that it is cash payable. Account deletion should disclose any pending redemption or unresolved balance before confirmation.

### 19.6 Fulfillment and program status

Tremendous and Tango are candidate providers, not selected partners. Verify actual merchant availability, country restrictions, denominations, funding fees, terms, and integration support before real issuance. A $5 Amazon card is a design assumption, not a confirmed provider minimum. Current official Tremendous information confirms Amazon gift-card distribution but says denominations vary by country. [3]

The prototype should use simulated points and fulfillment only. Real issuance needs appropriate account, transaction, and fraud controls plus review of the actual rewards rules and relevant platform requirements. Gift cards are not a blanket exemption from legal or platform obligations. Keep this implementation note in the handoff, not as a frightening warning repeated throughout the consumer experience.

## 20 Subscription and access design

Use $6.99 per month as the current working price and label it as a proposal in the design notes. Do not invent an annual price, a lifetime plan, an auto-renewing free trial, or a discount that was never decided.

The initial experience must let the user receive one personalized recommendation, inspect its evidence, choose a schedule, complete one real occurrence, and see that completion recorded before a blocking paywall. Do not gate Add to my plan, the starter habit's research, or its first completion behind payment. A user who wants to subscribe voluntarily can find pricing earlier, but it must not interrupt this journey.

Define the value moment as experiencing that complete first-habit journey once. Do not interpret full habit cycle as waiting until a health outcome appears, requiring a perfect week, or demanding a month of adherence. Let people complete the action in their own time; the prototype can simulate the passage of time without telling them to mark unfinished activity complete.

After the first completion and its acknowledgement, show a clear, dismissible invitation to continue with the paid experience. The proposed paid offering includes ongoing personalized routine management and deeper tracking; exact continuing free-versus-paid entitlements remain open. Preserve access to the starter record, evidence explanation, account controls, and support. Keep preview weekly-review and plan-adjustment states available in the design demonstration so the full journey can be assessed.

This paywall timing is a starting design hypothesis. Evaluate whether people understand the value and choose to subscribe; do not claim that a later paywall necessarily increases revenue. Rewards remain optional and should not be the only reason the core app appears worth paying for. Show pre-subscription reward illustrations as previews, with no redeemable credit awarded unless a separately funded promotion is approved.

The subscription screen needs a concise feature summary, price, billing interval, recurring-payment explanation, clear action, and links to terms and privacy. A demo must not pretend a real payment has been taken. If a native purchase route is shown, simulate restore-purchase and manage-subscription states with truthful labels.

Subscription management shows status, current billing period, renewal date, cancellation status, and a clear management route. Include payment failure, cancellation at period end, expired access, and return-to-paid states. Export, account deletion, support, and access to the user's existing rewards information should remain understandable.

Earlier structured courses or short guided habit programs can be represented as optional future paid content. Do not make a large course catalog mandatory for the first product or imply those courses already exist.

## 21 Settings and account screens

Profile and Goals lets the user edit name, goals, available time, preferences, and selected habits. Explain whether changing goals will suggest revisions rather than silently replacing an established plan.

Notifications supports per-habit reminders, quiet hours, time zone, pause reminders, and an optional weekly review. Offer frequency controls and a respectful adaptive suggestion such as Would evenings suit you better? Do not move schedules without consent or use guilt-based notifications. Provide a device-free or recurring quiet period option for people who want it.

Appearance and Accessibility supports text scaling, reduced motion, and a quieter illustration mode. A polished light theme is the priority. A dark theme is optional and should be complete if included, with independently checked contrast rather than automatic inversion.

Privacy and Data explains what is recorded, which sources are connected, and who can access it. Provide export, deletion, connection revocation, and a brief explanation of retention. Do not claim HIPAA certification, medical-device status, or other compliance credentials that have not been established.

Help includes how evidence grades work, recording habits, reminders, syncing, subscription questions, and rewards. Offer a content-error report with a habit or source already attached. Contact and feedback can be simulated in the prototype without actually sending messages.

Account screens include sign-in, sign-out, recovery, verification, authentication failure, and account deletion confirmation. Real credentials and payment details are outside the frontend demonstration.

## 22 Privacy and permissions as part of the experience

Keep required onboarding data minimal. Let the user browse and understand the app before requesting health-data access. Explain the benefit of each connection in plain language, distinguish optional from necessary information, and provide a continuing path after denial.

The design should make imported and manually logged data visible to the user. If data export is demonstrated, export only the sample dataset and label it clearly. Do not put actual sensitive health entries into an insecure public prototype or share them with unrelated external services.

Rewards fulfillment should receive only the information necessary to deliver the reward, not raw readings, mood logs, habit histories, or inferred medical conditions. Third-party AI, if later introduced, needs separate disclosure and an informed choice. [1][2]

## 23 Learning and discovery features

Preserve the option of short explanatory articles, instructional videos, and guided habit programs. A Learn or Read the science entry can live inside Discover rather than adding another main tab.

Content formats can include a habit explainer, a research update, a mechanism diagram, a beginner sequence, and a comparison of evidence for popular claims. Any scientific diagram is drawn in code from reviewed content; science graphics and charts are coded. Each needs an author or organization when real, review information, sources, and an appropriate reading time. Avoid an endless newsfeed that competes with completing the user's chosen plan.

Saved reading and saved habits should have distinct states. Saving an article does not create a scheduled habit. A new finding should not automatically change a user's routine without explanation.

## 24 Future concepts retained in the architecture

AI personalization or a conversational guide was an earlier expansion idea. A future guide should answer from the curated evidence library, expose its sources, acknowledge uncertainty, and help choose practical routines. It should not be a free-form diagnostic chatbot or a replacement for medical care. Design a concept entry and representative interaction without making generative AI necessary for the core app.

Community, accountability pods, private challenges, and coaching were also explored. Preserve them as optional future concepts. They should support participation and encouragement, with privacy controls and moderation appropriate to any sharing. The selected reward model does not depend on public health rankings, winner-takes-all competitions, or pooled wagers.

Continuous glucose monitoring is a future connection concept, not a default onboarding request. Expanded wearables and home devices, richer correlation analysis, clinician-facing exports, and advanced personalization can be mapped as later work. An export of user-selected records can be useful without representing a diagnostic report.

A growing avatar, category companions, geometric figures, and an illustrated world were earlier visual options. Preserve them as historical context only. The current build uses the consistent painted adult cast in Part Three, with all surrounding graphics coded. Do not add an evolving body or a nonhuman mascot. A future progress metaphor can be explored separately, but appearance must not become healthier-looking, thinner, or more valuable as the user earns points. The final app palette remains open.

Merchant-sponsored offers, subscription credits, free products, partner rewards, and a separately budgeted welcome bonus remain possible commercial experiments. Preserve them in the roadmap, not as promised launch inventory.

## 26 Accessibility and responsive behavior

Design for narrow phones through tablet and desktop. Suggested review widths are 360, 390, 430, 768, and 1440 CSS pixels, with an additional narrow and zoomed layout check. Preserve readable evidence labels, safe-area spacing, keyboard access, and usable navigation at every size.

Aim for at least 44 by 44 CSS-pixel touch areas on important controls as a product usability target. WCAG 2.2's target-size minimum is 24 by 24 CSS pixels with specified exceptions; larger controls are a deliberate design choice here. Ordinary text should meet at least 4.5:1 contrast and large text at least 3:1. Check actual combinations, including tinted cards and gradients. [4][5]

Use visible keyboard focus, semantic headings and controls, labels for icon buttons, meaningful alternative text where needed, and a logical reading order. Color cannot be the only indication of evidence level, completion, or error. Charts need accessible summaries and data values. Timed confirmations should remain understandable without requiring a fast response.

Respect reduced motion. Keep text selectable and real, including sources and numbers. Do not bake interface labels into illustration assets. Ensure dialogs are usable with a keyboard and screen reader; a bottom sheet should not leave focus behind it. Avoid a sticky action or bottom navigation covering the final paragraphs or the keyboard.

## 27 Coherent demonstration content

Use a fictional demonstration profile rather than the founder's real health records. Suggested profile: Alex, an adult with limited spare time who wants more consistent movement and sleep routines. Make its fictional status clear in the prototype controls.

Create enough library entries to demonstrate different categories, time commitments, evidence states, and filters. Twelve thoughtfully populated examples are preferable to hundreds of repetitive cards. Suggested topics are short walking, strength sessions, a consistent wind-down cue, a regular sleep schedule, reviewed breathing practice, movement breaks, mobility or posture, daylight exposure, hydration, a food-related habit, reading or reflection, and a research-only popular claim. Do not assign clinical claims or grades without verified content or an explicit demonstration label.

Provide distinct sample states: a new user with exactly one recommended starting habit and no history; that user after their first completion, when a subscription invitation becomes eligible; an established user with several weeks of entries; a missed-day return; rewards off; the paid-member ten-session rewards example at 3,200 points described in Section 19; and a separate redemption-ready example at 5,250 points. The last example redeems 5,000 points and leaves 250. Keep it separate from the 3,200-point profile so the interface does not jump between incompatible balances. The first-use journey must work without simulated payment, and its progress must not be confused with the paid reward balance.

A simple numerical progress fixture can show nine completed actions out of twelve due scheduled actions for a prior week, with three missed actions. The calendar and summary must agree. Chart data should contain deliberate gaps, source labels, and realistic variations without asserting that a habit caused a health change.

Use sample dates consistently across Today, the weekly review, subscription dates, reset dates, and the ledger. A demonstration-state selector can live in a discreet design-review menu, not in the normal consumer flow.

### 28.4 Honest local simulation
