# Make Loupe's Hero Show the Find

A from-scratch Loupe hero wins the first glance by **showing one act in the first frame: a designer types a description in trade language ("travertine side table, 1970s, warm") and gets back their own saved interiors, each with designer-vocabulary tags**. Around that sit a plain headline that says what Loupe does, one "free" call to action and a calm, conventional layout. The research gives three reasons. First, visitors judge two separate things on two clocks. Visual appeal forms in 17–50 ms and favours simple, familiar-looking pages. The stay-or-leave decision plays out over roughly the first 10 seconds and depends on whether visitors see their own problem and a credible fix. Second, the verified voice-of-customer evidence pins down the pain. Individual designers cannot get back to the *real* reference they saved, with its source, at the moment they need it, because Pinterest now buries saves under recommendations and fills feeds with AI rooms and ads. Studio owners face the same problem with an ownership twist: the studio's eye lives in individuals' phones and personal accounts, where a single ban or departure can erase it. Third, the competitive set leaves interiors-first positioning, designer-language tags shown on real images, studio-owned libraries and "out to Canva" unclaimed, while the 2026 design vocabulary has turned against the dark-glow "AI startup uniform" and against motion that hides content. The word "AI" belongs below the hero as the mechanism, never as the headline. Everything below is drawn from the research notes; where the verified second-pass notes corrected first-pass claims, the corrected versions are used, and the main corrections are flagged.

## Two clocks run at first glance, and most "first impression" lore misreads both

The strongest evidence on first impressions concerns *aesthetic appeal*, not comprehension or conversion. In Tuch et al. (2012, Google and the University of Basel), participants rated the beauty of masked screenshots of unfamiliar company homepages. **Visual complexity and prototypicality (how typical a page looks for its category) shaped ratings at exposures as short as 17 ms. Pages with low complexity and high prototypicality were rated most appealing**, and low-prototypicality pages were judged unattractive whether their complexity was high or low ([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/)). The verified manuscript corrects how this study is usually cited. Study 1 used 50/500/1000 ms (n = 59) and Study 2 used 17/33/50 ms (n = 82); there was no 17/50/500 design. Participants were young lay adults (Study 2's had "no education in either visual design or web design"), and the authors never measured stay or leave. The Google blog's line that users decide "whether they'll stay or leave" within 50 ms is framing, not a result ([Tuch et al. manuscript](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/38315.pdf); [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs-why-websites-need-to-make-a-great-first-impression/)). Related work points the same way: 50 ms appeal ratings track longer-exposure ratings ([Tractinsky et al. 2006](https://typeset.io/papers/evaluating-the-consistency-of-immediate-aesthetic-kw752b0u25)), and complexity matters more than colourfulness, with effects that vary by demographic ([Reinecke et al., CHI 2013](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2013/reinecke13predicting.shtml)).

The second clock is relevance. NN/g's reading of Microsoft Research's dwell-time data (more than 2 billion visits, about 2010) found that **leaving is most likely in the first 10 seconds; the curve flattens after about 30 seconds**, and "to gain several minutes of user attention, you must clearly communicate your value proposition within 10 seconds" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)). That figure describes where a general-browsing hazard curve is steepest. It is not a tested landing-page rule. Read it as "the hero must answer *what is this and is it for me* almost at once", and note the corollary: a visitor who reaches the second screen has passed the harshest filter, so that screen deserves real work.

Attention concentrates high on the page, but less than the old numbers claim. **NN/g's 2018 desktop eyetracking (120 participants, 1920×1080) found 57% of viewing time above the fold and 74% in the first two screenfuls.** The often-quoted 80% figure comes from 2010 data on 1024×768 monitors, and neither figure is mobile data ([NN/g 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/); [NN/g 2010](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention-original-research/)). NN/g's 2024 guidance still treats the fold as a "gatekeeper" on every device. It explicitly warns about "false floors" in image-led minimal heroes, where a full-bleed picture gives no cue that content continues ([NN/g 2024](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/)).

Credibility, for lay users, is read largely from design. In the Stanford Web Credibility Project, "design look" was the most-mentioned factor (46.1% of comments), with experts far less swayed than consumers ([Consumer Reports WebWatch](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/how-do-people-evaluate-a-web-sites-credibility)). Loupe's audience inverts that split: for interior designers, visual craft *is* domain content. A clumsy hero will likely read as "this tool doesn't understand my work", though no study has tested designers as a population. Processing fluency explains why plain wins. Easy-to-process stimuli feel more beautiful ([Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman 2004](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkielman/reber-schwarz-winkielman-beauty-PSPR-2004.pdf)), and instructions printed in a hard-to-read font made the *task* seem nearly twice as long ([Song & Schwarz 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19000208/)). A busy hero makes Loupe itself look like work.

The evidence sorts popular claims as follows.

| Claim | Verdict | What the evidence supports |
|---|---|---|
| "You have 50 ms to make an impression" | Real but narrow | Applies to visual appeal only, rated by lay users on corporate homepages ([Tuch et al.](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/38315.pdf)) |
| "8-second attention span, shorter than a goldfish" | Myth | Traces to a marketing report citing no original research ([WARC](https://www.warc.com/newsandopinion/opinion/the_goldfish_myth/2806)) |
| "Users don't scroll" / "80% above the fold" | Outdated | 57% first screen, 74% first two (desktop, 2018) ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)) |
| "Loss framing is twice as persuasive" | Overstated | Gain vs loss message framing differs by about r = .03 across 93 studies ([O'Keefe & Jensen 2007](https://healthyskepticism.org/global/library/item/12801)) |
| "More choice kills conversion" | Overstated | Mean choice-overload effect "virtually zero" across 50 experiments ([Scheibehenne et al. 2010](https://abcwest.cogs.indiana.edu/pmwiki/pdf/scheibehenne.options.2010.pdf)) |
| "Beautiful = perceived usable" | Contested | Experiment found poor usability lowers beauty ratings, not the reverse ([Tuch et al. 2012](https://research.google/pubs/is-beautiful-really-usable-toward-understanding-the-relation-between-usability-aesthetics-and-affect-in-hci/)) |
| "Free" changes behaviour | Strong | A drop to zero shifts choice far more than an equal price cut ([Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely 2007](https://people.duke.edu/~dandan/webfiles/PapersPI/Zero%20as%20a%20Special%20Price.pdf)) |
| Carousels show more messages | Weak | People scroll past them; NN/g recommends a static hero ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designing-effective-carousels/)) |
| Red/orange buttons convert best | Myth | Contrast with the page matters, not hue ([Atticus Li](https://atticusli.com/blog/posts/cta-button-ab-tests-why-button-color-tests-are-worthless/)) |

The pain-versus-benefit debate matters less than founders assume. Message-framing meta-analyses find valence barely moves persuasion. Negativity bias does mean concrete pain grabs attention ([Baumeister et al. 2001](https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/Baumeister_2001.pdf)), and fear appeals work on average (d = 0.29), especially when paired with **efficacy, a clear doable remedy** ([APA on Tannenbaum et al. 2015](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2015/10/fear-based-appeals)). In field data, curiosity-gap headlines drew fewer views than plain summaries ([Mapping Ignorance on Scacco & Muddiman](https://mappingignorance.org/?p=6526)). The recipe is to name the pain only if the remedy is visible on the same screen, and never to withhold what the product is. Problem-agitation-solution has no peer-reviewed evidence base.

On imagery, NN/g's eyetracking shows people scrutinise information-carrying photos of products and real people and ignore "big feel-good images that are purely decorative" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)). NN/g tells SaaS products to show "clear screenshots of their software" and warns that ten similar visuals above the fold overwhelm the eye ([NN/g 2024](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/7-tips-memorable-imagery/)). A new August 2026 NN/g study found AI-generated hero images no worse than *stock* photos when viewers weren't told. But when participants suspected AI, they rated the site lower ([NN/g 2026](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-generated-images/)), a real risk with an audience trained to spot "AI glow". Mobile speed is a first-order risk too. **Google's 2016 benchmark (n = 3.7K mobile sites) found 53% of visits are "likely to be abandoned" past 3 seconds** ([Google](https://blog.google/products/ads-commerce/the-need-for-mobile-speed/)). The often-quoted "+32% bounce from 1 s to 3 s" is a *modelled relative* increase from Google/SOASTA's 2017 neural net. That same model linked going from 400 to 6,000 page elements with a 95% drop in conversion probability, the more relevant warning for an image-heavy page ([Think with Google PDF](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/57/mobile-page-speed-new-industry-benchmarks.pdf)). Both figures are nearly ten years old and come from an interested party.

## The real pain: designers can't get back to the real reference, studios don't own their eye

The verified pass corrected several first-pass quotes. Most press claims stand, but with different wording or speakers. For example, Business of Home's Pinterest roundup is from 2019, and the line about clients' Pinterest pages belongs to Kevin Isbell ("I cringe when a client wants to send me a Pinterest page"), not Claudia Martin. The "chairs defying physics" line is Wired's, not Tom's Guide's. And the FT quote, the "library of Alexandria" lead, and the garden designer's "insane looking structures" line cannot be verified and should not be used ([verification sources: BOH 2019](https://businessofhome.com/articles/how-do-you-really-feel-about-pinterest); [Wired](https://www.wired.com/story/pinterst-ai-slop-content/)). What survives is stronger and more current than the first pass suggested.

### Individual designers: "Where are the rest of my 1,000 plus pins?"

The individual designer's pain has three layers. The newest is that **Pinterest's own late-2026 updates push recommendations in front of users' saves**. App Store reviewers write: "Went into 'saved' pins and clicked on a board. Does it take me to my saved? No. Take me to 'more ideas'… Where are the rest of my 1,000 plus pins???" (27 Oct 2026). Another writes that after an update they "can no longer search for the board I want and have to scroll through dozens of boards" (4 Nov 2026) ([Pinterest App Store feed](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/id=429047995/sortby=mostrecent/json)). An earlier mymind reviewer switched because "I can't find any of my 'saves' on Pinterest without searching forever" ([mymind App Store feed](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/id=1520332347/sortby=mostrecent/json)).

The second layer is trust in what is real. People using Pinterest for interiors now write that "it is now almost entirely AI content. I've had it for more than 10 years and am now deleting it" (20 Oct 2026). Another says, "I want to see real images of real homes" (23 Aug 2026) ([Pinterest US feed](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/id=429047995/sortby=mostrecent/json); [UK feed](https://itunes.apple.com/gb/rss/customerreviews/id=429047995/sortby=mostrecent/json)). Wired found **over 40% of the first 73 pins on a fresh account were ads**, and Pinterest's AI labels appear only after a click and not on ads ([Wired, 24 Dec 2025](https://www.wired.com/story/pinterst-ai-slop-content/)). For professionals the damage reaches clients. Kelly and Graeme Smith of SPELK write that clients "arrive with fully-formed visuals of their rooms, created by AI… 'Can you make that?'" and bring "boards where they've unknowingly pinned AI images created by others" ([Livingetc, Oct 2026](https://www.livingetc.com/features/the-truth-about-ai-homes)). An artist quoted by 404 Media sums up the cost: "it sucks to constantly have to question and check if something is authentic or synthetic" ([404 Media, Feb 2026](https://www.404media.co/pinterest-is-drowning-in-a-sea-of-ai-slop-and-auto-moderation/)).

The third layer is context and fragility. A Cosmos reviewer wants to "call out" why she saved something rather than "download the image, go to the Notes app… which gets really messy" ([Cosmos feed, Nov 2026](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/id=1577975475/sortby=mostrecent/json)). Brooklyn designer Melissa Lee wants to "credit the right people" for inspiration images ([Business of Home, Mar 2026](https://businessofhome.com/articles/cosmos-wants-to-be-more-than-just-pinterest-for-the-cool-kids)). And platforms delete libraries: Pinterest admitted that "an internal error led to over-enforcement and some accounts were mistakenly deactivated" ([TechCrunch, May 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/05/14/pinterest-finally-admits-mass-bans-were-a-mistake-caused-by-an-internal-error/)). Designers also struggle with vocabulary. "Most clients can't speak our design language fluently," says Wesley Moon, and Darren Henault adds, "Your 'traditional' is another person's 'transitional'" ([Business of Home, 2020](https://businessofhome.com/articles/how-useful-are-inspiration-images)). Generic AI tagging misses this: one mymind user found tags "too broad to be useful" for specialist material (vendor-reported, [Saner.ai](https://www.saner.ai/blogs/mymind-reviews)).

Put together, **the individual designer cannot reliably get back to the real reference they saved, with its source and the reason they saved it, at the moment they need it**. They feel it when scrolling the phone after a market trip ("what vendor was this"), when building a scheme and not trusting what's real, when a client arrives with AI rooms, and when an account vanishes. One honest gap remains: no named interior designer was found saying the exact words "I know I saved it somewhere". That phrase is a strong hypothesis built from adjacent quotes, which customer interviews should confirm.

### Studio owners: "a decade of research… essential to my projects and collaboration with my team"

The best single piece of studio-level evidence is a design firm's Pinterest suspension thread. The owner had held the account "for over 10 years and use it daily as part of my design firm's professional work". It held "a decade of research, design selections, and creative references that are essential to my projects and collaboration with my team", and three appeals were rejected ([Pinterest Community, Nov 2025](https://community.pinterest.biz/t/my-pinterest-account-was-suspended-by-mistake-10-years-of-professional-work-lost/40372)). Principals' language about what they want comes from CLOTH & KIND's founders, in a vendor-hosted webinar, so it is indicative rather than neutral. They call a shared library "our single source of digital truth for design… a shared resource across our two offices" and say "we don't have to start over". They also describe trade shows: "taking pictures, pictures, pictures, and having to go through your phone later… Oh, gosh, what vendor was this" ([StyleRow webinar transcript](https://intercom.help/stylerow/en/articles/10035435-building-new-efficiencies-in-the-design-process)).

The fragility comes from people as much as platforms. Anissa Zajac says that after her first employee left, "the rest of the team kind of fell apart" ([Business of Home](https://businessofhome.com/articles/how-this-indiana-designer-built-a-process-for-making-great-hires)). Coach Sean Low warns that "if your firm will flounder because an employee is leaving… you are a brokerage" ([Business of Home](https://businessofhome.com/articles/business-advice-transition-after-employee-gives-notice)). Michael Cox describes onboarding through "inspirationsharing sessions" ([Business of Home](https://businessofhome.com/boh/article/the-mentor)). Most firms are tiny: **64% are sole practitioners or have one or two employees** ([Pearl Collective 2024 survey](https://pearlprograms.s3.amazonaws.com/2024-survey/2024_INTERIOR_DESIGN_BUSINESS_SURVEY_FINDINGS_INSIGHT_REPORT.pdf)). So the studio owner's pain is "the studio's eye lives in people's phones and accounts, so it isn't ours". Because the owner is usually also the lead designer, it is best presented as the designer's pain plus ownership, continuity and sharing. One caution: no principal was found saying that *image references* specifically leave with departing staff. That link is an inference to validate.

There is no interior-design survey of time spent searching for images. The defensible general figure is **Atlassian's 2025 finding that teams "waste 25%" of their time searching for answers** (12,000 knowledge workers) ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/state-of-teams-2025)). Canto's creative-team framing, "when content lives everywhere, it lives nowhere", mirrors the designer evidence ([Canto 2026](https://www.canto.com/resources/state-of-digital-content/)). Any "designers lose X hours" claim needs Loupe's own survey.

## The 2026 heroes that work show the product doing its one job

Across 16 admired SaaS and creative heroes captured on 8 November 2026, the formula is consistent. **Headlines run about 5–6 words at the median, there is one primary call to action that names the action and its cost, and a product visual does something within 2–5 seconds. Social proof almost never appears above the fold** (only Notion's did), and logos arrive in the first scroll. Granola is the closest model for Loupe. Rough meeting notes type in, an "Enhancing notes" state turns them into structured notes, and the subhead "Notes, actions and memory. Without a meeting bot." contrasts the product with the alternative the buyer already knows ([Granola](https://www.granola.ai/); `granola_hero_desktop.png`). Notion and Figma use rotating words and cycling output cards (`notion-desktop.png`, `figma_hero_desktop.png`). A commercial teardown of Linear praised its single decisive call to action and sub-1 s LCP but faulted it for stating a category without an outcome ([Landing Doctor](https://landingdoctors.com/teardowns/linear-app)).

Loupe's direct category has settled on one hero shape: a very large type headline, one line of subhead, a dark "…for free" pill plus a light secondary pill, a minimal nav and, since 2025, a floating QR "Get the app" card. Cosmos makes search itself the hero, with a rotating H1 ("Find your next lamp with character… forever chair… room-defining piece"), a live search bar and "Join Cosmos for free" ([Cosmos](https://www.cosmos.so/); `cosmos_hero_desktop.png`). Savee sets saved images inline between headline words above a masonry wall (`savee_hero_desktop.png`). mymind pairs a giant serif "Remember everything. Organize nothing." with three platform pills: iPhone app, Browser Extension, Android app (`mymind_hero_desktop.png`). Interiors software looks different. Programa builds a tactile collage of swatches around a monitor showing its UI, with the trade named outright ("FOR INTERIOR DESIGNERS & ARCHITECTS") (`programa_hero_desktop.png`, `programa_hero_mobile.png`), while incumbents such as DesignFiles, Studio Designer and Houzz Pro lead with admin relief, long headlines and demo calls to action ([Houzz Pro](https://pro.houzz.com/for-pros/software-interior-designer); `houzz-pro-desktop.png`).

The gaps are as instructive. **No competitor is interiors-first, none shows AI output in trade vocabulary, and none promises that the studio owns the library.** refern shows tags, but generic ones (#sunset) (`refern-desktop.png`). Savee's demo search is "burnt orange". Savee's "a space that stays the team's own" refers to team branding, not retention ([Savee](https://savee.com/)). No inspiration tool names Canva. The closest line in the whole set is Fabric's "Designers → A visual library that finds anything by description, colour, or mood" ([Fabric](https://fabric.so/)), a warning that "find by describing" alone is no longer ownable. Cosmos now ships AI "research captions" and colour sorting and courts interiors firms ([Business of Home](https://businessofhome.com/articles/cosmos-wants-to-be-more-than-just-pinterest-for-the-cool-kids)). Loupe's differentiation therefore has to rest on the *combination*: designer-language tags, a private studio-owned library, capture from Chrome and iPhone, zip export, and drag into Canva. Tools that pitched mainly on AI organising have pivoted or closed: Kosmik "will sunset on May 31st 2026" and Kive now sells AI product photography ([Kosmik](https://www.kosmik.app/); `kive_hero_desktop.png`).

### Visual trends: look like interiors, not like AI

The most useful 2026 trend finding is negative. Setproduct catalogues the "AI startup uniform": a background near #0A0A0A, a purple-to-blue glow, a sparkle icon, an "Introducing" pill, Inter or Geist alone, a bento grid, glass panels and typing effects. "None of these is a crime on its own. Stacked together they become a fingerprint" ([Setproduct, Sep 2026](https://www.setproduct.com/blog/why-every-ai-startup-looks-the-same)). Eagle wears it inside Loupe's own category (`eagle_hero_desktop.png`). Even the "tasteful" alternative of cream plus terracotta is now flagged as an AI default by an open-source audit tool ([GitHub: avoid-ai-design](https://github.com/funboy322/avoid-ai-design)). That matters because Loupe's current peach accent (#EF8A66) sits near that family and its near-black ground (#0e0e0e) sits near the uniform. Rising instead are expressive serif display type (mymind, Granola, Programa, Affinity), real output in the hero, and the visual grammar of the customer's domain. Heron's technical-drawing site for architects dropped the word "AI" from its brief ([Codrops](https://tympanus.net/codrops/2026/10/12/heron-ai/)), and Programa's material collage does the same for interiors. Agencies report "decorative AI visuals… aging badly" and recommend one light or material effect per page ([Digidop](https://www.digidop.com/blog/5-web-design-trends-2025)).

Light versus dark has no conversion data. The direct category (Cosmos, Savee, mymind, Are.na) is light, NN/g's literature review finds light mode reads better for most users ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/)), and dark aligns Loupe with the uniform. A dark page can still work if it behaves like a gallery wall, as Affinity's Webby-recognised black ground with giant serif type and floating real work shows (`affinity_hero_desktop.png`). My recommendation is a light-to-mid neutral "plaster and paper" ground carried by material photography, with peach kept for tags and one call to action. If brand equity forces dark, it should read as darkroom, not terminal.

### Motion is now cheap, so only purposeful motion stands out

Motion tooling became free or native in 2025–2026. **GSAP, including SplitText and ScrollTrigger, went 100% free in April 2025** ([Webflow](https://webflow.com/blog/gsap-becomes-free)), and **CSS scroll-driven animations reached Baseline in October 2026** ([web.dev](https://web.dev/blog/web-platform-10-2026)). They run off the main thread; one Chrome case study reports CPU while scrolling falling from 50% to 2% ([Chrome](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/css-ui-ecommerce-sda)). Because AI builders now generate GSAP effects, motion alone no longer differentiates; a single recognisable motion signature does.

The usability evidence sets hard limits. NN/g found scrolljacking "leads to more user pain than benefits". It is worse on mobile and worst when combined with text, and users sometimes read long scrolljacks "as a bug". If used at all, it should sit below the fold ([NN/g 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/)). Parallax impresses "other designers or developers" while users stay indifferent ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/parallax-usability/)). Moving homepage elements "are often assumed to be ads", and the headline should never be animated into view ([NN/g 2024](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/)). Evidence on animated versus static heroes is agency-grade only, but it points one way. Motion that demonstrates the offer lifted order completions 7% in one Cro Metrics test ([Cro Metrics](https://crometrics.com/blog/animated-vs-static-hero-banners-cro-show/)). Decorative video heroes lost about 7% against static across 40+ Shopify builds, and video without a poster pushed mobile LCP to 3.2–4.8 s ([WitsCode](https://witscode.com/blogs/shopify-homepage-hero)). Fade-ins from opacity 0 can delay LCP because Chrome ignores invisible paints ([DebugBear](https://www.debugbear.com/blog/opacity-animation-poor-lcp)). Jitter's and Linear's heroes rendered blank in headless captures seconds after load (`jitter_hero_desktop.png`, `linear_hero_mobile.png`), exactly the "dies at first glance" failure Loupe must avoid. Accessibility rules are concrete. WCAG 2.2.2 (Level A) requires a pause control for auto-moving content longer than 5 seconds, which covers rotating headline words and marquees ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)), and honouring `prefers-reduced-motion` is standard practice ([W3C 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)).

## Copy that wins: say the mechanism, keep "AI" out of the headline, put "free" in the button

The best-supported copy finding is that **clear beats clever**, though the lifts come from single vendor case studies. MECLABS replaced "You can't be too careful" with a plain value statement and reports 330% more conversions ([MarketingExperiments](https://marketingexperiments.com/value-proposition/clarity-trumps-persuasion)). Pages at a 5th–7th grade reading level had a median conversion of 11.1%, against 5.3% at professional level, a correlation rather than proof ([Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/methodology/)). Nielsen's front-loading guidance holds that scanners take in roughly the first 11 characters ([Wylie Communications](https://www.wyliecomm.com/web-page-headlines/)). NN/g's tests for a tagline ask whether it explains what the company does and whether a competitor could say it equally well ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tagline-blues-whats-the-site-about/)); cheerful "welcomes" fail them ([NN/g 2024](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/)). Kohavi's data is a reminder that most tested ideas fail, with a median success rate near 10% ([Lenny Distilled](https://lennydistilled.pages.dev/guests/ronny-kohavi)), so borrowed lifts are direction, not forecasts.

Loupe's current H1, "Never lose the one you know you saw.", sounds like the customer and names a real feeling. But it does not say what Loupe is, so clarity rests on a small eyebrow and a 33-word subhead, roughly twice the length of any comparable live subhead (which run 7–18 words). mymind already owns the generic "never lose anything" register ("Remember everything. Organize nothing."). The strongest Loupe claim is **mechanism plus outcome in designer words: you find a saved image by describing it the way a designer would.** It can be pictured, the demo can prove it, and it passes NN/g's competitor test better than "beautifully organized". Candidate headlines, in the order to test them, are "Find any image you saved by describing it." and "Describe it. Loupe finds it." as clarity leads, with "You saw it once. Now you can find it." as the pain-led variant. That variant is acceptable only if the first words of the subhead state the mechanism. The ownable contrast line, "Pinterest sees 'orange.' You see terracotta.", is better as the subhead's second sentence or the second-screen headline than as the H1. A 15–22-word subhead fits: "Save from Chrome or iPhone. Loupe tags every image in designer language, so you can find it again by describing it."

The evidence on AI wording is unusually consistent. In experimental surveys of more than 1,000 US adults, **mentioning "AI" in product descriptions lowered purchase intention through reduced emotional trust** ([WSU on Cicek, Gursoy & Lu 2024](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2024/07/30/using-the-term-artificial-intelligence-in-product-descriptions-reduces-purchase-intentions/)). In 2026, 60% of US consumers called AI in brand messaging a turnoff ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/16/sixty-percent-of-u-s-consumers-say-ai-in-brand-messaging-is-a-turnoff-survey-finds/)). Designers are split: 78% say AI speeds them up, but only 58% say it improves quality ([Figma AI report 2025](https://figma.com/reports/ai-2025/)). NN/g's 2025 verdict is blunt: "AI is a technology, not a user benefit" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/powered-by-ai-is-not-a-value-proposition/)). There are caveats in Loupe's favour. Loupe's AI is *analytic*, describing your own saves rather than generating content, and labels reading "AI-assisted" fare better than "AI-generated" ([MDPI 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/0718-1876/21/5/154)). Even so, the backlash lands on tools that merely use AI: Cosmos users plead "PLEASE DO NOT do ANYTHING with AI I BEG", and Milanote is praised for having none ([Cosmos App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id1577975475); [Milanote App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id1433852790)). So "AI" stays out of the H1 and subhead and appears once lower down as the mechanism, with proof and control: "Loupe doesn't make images. It helps you find yours." and "See and edit every tag." This answers Fabric users' complaint that they "can't remove AI created tags" ([Fabric App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id6449003046)). Add a training or privacy line only if it is true.

For the call to action, the zero-price effect makes "free" Loupe's strongest lever, and live leaders put it *in the button* ("Get Notion free", "Join Cosmos for free", "Download for free"), not the headline. One study, Aagaard's first-person "Start my free 30 day trial", is the only evidence for "my" over "your" (+90% click-through) ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/article/269879)). Reassurance next to the button lifted trial sign-ups 12.5% in one VWO test ([VWO/Teamleader](https://www.casestudies.com/company/vwo/case-study/teamleader-increased-free-trial-sign-ups-through-rigorous-ab-testing)). Loupe's "Free to use — not a trial." is distinctive against interiors software that wraps a 7-day trial in "Start free" (Programa, `programa-desktop.png`). It should stay, with "No card" added. "Free forever" should be avoided unless the limits sit beside it, because designers have learned to distrust it: one mymind reviewer called the push to pay "just to keep using what you've already saved" a "trap" ([mymind App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id1520332347)). For an install-led product, the real next step can be the button itself, as mymind and Granola show.

## A blueprint for Loupe's hero and the page beneath it

The evidence converges on a hero that is **structurally conventional, distinctive in art direction, fully painted in the first frame and animated only on top of visible content**. The table sets out the specification, with the evidence behind each choice.

| Element | Recommendation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Eyebrow | "For interior designers and studios" | Names the trade; no competitor is interiors-first |
| H1 | Mechanism plus outcome, 5–8 words: "Find any image you saved by describing it." Test against the pain variant | Clarity beats cleverness; front-loading; Linear critique |
| Subhead | 15–22 words: capture surfaces, then designer-language tagging, then "find it by describing it"; optional contrast "Pinterest sees orange. You see terracotta." | Live subheads run 7–18 words; Granola-style contrast |
| Primary call to action | Context-aware: desktop "Add to Chrome — free", iPhone "Get the iPhone app — free"; baseline "Start free" | Zero-price effect; mymind and Granola make install the button |
| Secondary | One quiet "See how it works" | Avoid equally salient competing calls to action ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-impressions-human-automaticity/)) |
| Microcopy | "Free to use — not a trial. No card. Chrome, iPhone and web." | Removes cost and trial anxiety; differentiates from Programa |
| Visual | Real Loupe UI holding 4–6 real interior images (not a 30-tile wall); a designer query types in and saved images return with tag chips such as "travertine", "bouclé", "1970s Italian", "warm minimal" | Information-carrying imagery; low complexity; "show the output, not the orb" |
| Motion | One 3–5 s demo loop that plays once and then rests, with a pause control; one signature (a lens or "found" marker); no opacity-0 start on H1 or hero image | NN/g headline rule; WCAG 2.2.2; LCP mechanics |
| Fold | A visible cut-off of the next section | NN/g false-floor warning |
| Avoid | Carousels, autoplay video as LCP, glow, sparkle icons, "Introducing" pill, "AI-powered", small user counts, cookie banner over the mobile call to action | Uniform; carousel and video evidence; Powell et al. on small counts |
| Mobile | H1, one-line sub, one call to action and a glimpse of tagged images within 844 px; no pinning; the iPhone share sheet is the hero path | NN/g mobile guidance; category convention |
| Performance | Hero images AVIF/WebP, about 150–300 KB, explicit dimensions; lazy-loaded galleries; preloaded font | Google speed data; SOASTA element count |

The second screen deserves a strong idea, because attention flattens after 30 seconds and 74% of viewing falls in the first two screens. It should host a **try-it moment: tappable preset queries ("terracotta bouclé, 70s", "limewash bedroom, linen") that always return beautiful results**. The IKEA-effect research shows effort creates value only when it succeeds, and builders who failed to finish lost the effect ([Norton, Mochon & Ariely 2012](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/12136084/norton%20mochon%20ariely%20third%20round[1].pdf)), so a free-text box that can return nothing is riskier than curated chips. Vendor demo data is suggestive only ([Navattic](https://navattic.com/report/state-of-the-interactive-product-demo-2025)). A short pinned "watch an image get tagged" sequence is defensible here on desktop alone, under NN/g's rules: one sequence, little text and no horizontal hijack. On mobile it should become a normal-scroll stack, with scroll-linked reveals built on CSS `view()` timelines and a GSAP fallback that respects reduced motion.

Below that, the page should follow the pain map. A "your saves, not suggestions" section answers the October–November 2026 Pinterest complaints directly and shows source and notes attached to each save; it is timely, evidence-backed, and positions Loupe against a shared enemy rather than blaming users' "messy folders". A capture section shows Chrome and the iPhone share sheet in one line. A studio section speaks in principals' own register: "One library your studio owns. When a designer leaves, the library stays." This draws on "single source of digital truth" and "we don't have to start over", and no competitor makes the promise. A section on getting images out of Loupe ("drag into Canva or any canvas; download any board as a zip") answers lock-in fears, which the bans made salient, and claims the canvas bridge no competitor names. A short "how the tagging works" section is where AI is named, plainly, with editable tags. For social proof, the evidence favours **specific, named testimonials from real studios** over counts. People treat review quantity as popularity even irrationally ([Powell et al. 2017](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2017/09/online-consumers-choose-inferior-products)), so "Join 37 designers" hurts. Dynamic norms ("more studios are moving their libraries each week") can persuade even a minority, and should be used only if true ([Sparkman & Walton 2017](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2017/10/change-behaviors-changing-perception-normal)). The citable outside facts that hold up are Wired's ad count, TechCrunch's "internal error" admission, the design-firm ban thread and 404 Media's quotes, used in their verified wording.

Validation should come before conviction. With Kohavi's failure rates and probably modest traffic, Loupe should run 5-second and "what does this do?" clarity tests with 15–30 interior designers on two or three hero variants before A/B testing. It should measure activation (first save, first search) rather than button clicks, and keep ads in message match with the winning headline. Interviews should probe the two inferences the evidence could not close: whether designers use the phrase "I know I saved it", and whether principals see references leaving with staff.

## Conclusion

The research shifts the question from "how do we grab attention" to "how fast can we prove the find". The aesthetic snap judgment is won by restraint and familiarity, not spectacle. The relevance judgment is won by the visitor recognising their own moment: the phone after a market trip, the client with an AI room, the saved board that now shows "more ideas". The moment has to be met with a visible remedy in the same frame. A real interior image gaining real designer tags, then coming back from a described search, does both jobs at once, and it is the one asset competitors cannot copy without also adopting the trade's vocabulary.

Two strategic implications go beyond the hero. First, the market has punished "AI organises your stuff" as a pitch (Kosmik, Kive) and is fatigued by the word, while Pinterest's AI turn has made authenticity and ownership the live emotional terrain. Loupe should therefore position as the *anti-feed*: your real references, credited, in a library your studio owns, and exportable whenever you like. AI stays the quiet engine of that promise. Second, "find by describing" is already being claimed by horizontal tools (Fabric, Cosmos), so the moat is vertical depth. Loupe can only win the first glance for as long as its tags sound like a designer and its studio story sounds like a principal, which makes tag quality in the demo a product priority, not just a marketing one.
