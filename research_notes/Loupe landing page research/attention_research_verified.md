## 1. NN/g "How Long Do Users Stay on Web Pages?" (the 10-second finding and the hazard curve)

### Takeaway
**Verified.** Jakob Nielsen published the article on **11 September 2011**. It reinterprets Liu, White & Dumais (Microsoft Research, SIGIR 2010), which fitted more than 2 billion dwell times from a browser plug-in. The headline guidance is quoted exactly: "To gain several minutes of user attention, you must clearly communicate your value proposition within 10 seconds." The data comes from general web browsing around 2010. It does not come from landing pages or from a test NN/g ran itself.

### Cited Findings
- **Verified, byline and date:** Jakob Nielsen, "How Long Do Users Stay on Web Pages?", dated September 11, 2011. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)
- **Verified, summary wording:** "Users often leave Web pages in 10–20 seconds, but pages with a clear value proposition can hold people's attention for much longer. To gain several minutes of user attention, you must clearly communicate your value proposition within 10 seconds." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)
- **Verified:** "The average page visit lasts a little less than a minute." Users "have time to read only a quarter of the text on the pages they actually visit." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)
- **New (this closes a first-pass gap on dataset size):** NN/g describes the data source as follows. "Research by Chao Liu and colleagues from Microsoft Research … collected data from 'a popular web browser plug-in,' analyzing page-visit durations for 205,873 different web pages for which they had captured upwards of 10,000 visits … (more than 2 billion dwell times)." Result: "the time users spend on a web page follows a Weibull distribution." The reference given is Liu, White & Dumais, "Understanding web browsing behaviors through Weibull analysis of dwell time," SIGIR '10. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)
- **Verified:** "The researchers discovered that 99% of web pages have a negative aging effect." NN/g's explanation: "users … spend their initial time on a page in ruthless triage to abandon the dross ASAP." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)
- **Verified, hazard-curve claims:** The chart plots "the hazard function — that is, the likelihood of leaving — for the median Weibull parameters fitted across the scientists' humongous dataset." The text says: "the first 10 seconds of the page visit are critical for users' decision to stay or leave … If the web page survives this first — extremely harsh — 10-second judgment, users will look around a bit. However, they're still highly likely to leave during the subsequent 20 seconds of their visit. Only after people have stayed on a page for about 30 seconds does the curve become relatively flat." It also says: "if you can convince users to stay on your page for half a minute, there's a fair chance that they'll stay much longer — often 2 minutes or more." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)
- **Verified, interpretation caveat:** NN/g states that "'good' vs. 'bad' is a decision that each individual user makes within those first few seconds of arriving." This is NN/g's reading of the curve. Liu et al. modeled leave times; they did not measure why users judged a page good or bad. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)
- **New, related older NN/g guidance:** In "Tagline Blues" (21 July 2001), Nielsen had already written: "Users decide quickly whether to stay or leave a site. To assess whether your homepage communicates effectively to visitors in the crucial first 10 seconds, follow two simple guidelines." — [NN/g: Tagline Blues](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tagline-blues-whats-the-site-about/)

### Inferences
- The 10-second figure is an NN/g reading of where a browsing hazard curve is steepest. It is not a tested "10-second rule" for landing pages. Treat it as "the hero must answer 'what is this and is it for me?' almost at once." Do not treat it as a precise budget.
- The curve flattens after about 30 seconds. That supports making the second screen strong, because a visitor who scrolls past the hero has passed the harshest filter.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the original SIGIR 2010 paper. Dataset details (plug-in, 205,873 pages, more than 2 billion dwell times) are as NN/g reports them. The data date from about 2009–2010 and cover all browsing, not marketing landing pages.

## 2. NN/g scrolling and attention: first-screen and two-screen share of viewing time (2010 vs 2018), and what NN/g says about the fold today

### Takeaway
**Verified, with corrections on device and method.** In 2010 NN/g measured **80.3%** of viewing time above the fold, on a **1024×768 desktop monitor** (21 users, 541 pages, 57,453 fixations). In 2018 it measured **about 57%** above the fold and **74%** in the first two screenfuls, on a **1920×1080 desktop screen** (120 participants, over 130,000 fixations). Both studies were desktop eyetracking, not mobile. NN/g's current position (2024 homepage guidance, 2023 mobile report) is that the fold "remains crucial" on every device.

### Cited Findings
- **Verified, 2010 figure, with more precision:** "Scrolling and Attention (Original Research Study)," Jakob Nielsen, March 21, 2010. Summary: "Web users spend 80% of their time looking at information above the page fold. Although users do scroll, only 20% of their attention goes to content below the fold." The exact split was "Above the fold: 80.3%; Below the fold: 19.7%." Method: "I analyzed data from only 21 users accessing 541 different web pages … a total of 57,453 fixations," from unconstrained tasks, and "we used a screen resolution of 1,024 × 768 pixels." The page now carries a note: "Update: With newer data from eyetracking on bigger monitors." — [NN/g 2010](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention-original-research/)
- **Verified, 2018 figures:** "Scrolling and Attention," Therese Fessenden, April 15, 2018: "users spent about 57% of their page-viewing time above the fold. 74% of the viewing time was spent in the first two screenfuls, up to 2160px." Also: "the pattern of a sharp decrease in attention following the fold remains the same in 2018 as in 2010." — [NN/g 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- **Corrected, device and sample:** The first pass flagged the device as uncertain, and a CT.gov guide calls it a mobile finding. NN/g's method statement settles it: "we analyzed the x, y coordinates of over 130,000 eye fixations on a 1920×1080 screen. These fixations were from 120 participants … including news, ecommerce, blogs, FAQs, and encyclopedic pages," compared with "our previous eyetracking study on 1024×768 monitors." This is desktop data. NN/g adds: "We can't tease apart the relative impact of these two changes [bigger screens; new design trends]." — [NN/g 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- **New, 2018 detail:** "the second screenful of content received about a third of that (17% viewing time); the remaining 26% was spread in a long-tail distribution." Also: "today, 81% of the viewing time is spent in the first three screenfuls," and "People still don't scroll a lot — they rarely go beyond the third screenful of info." — [NN/g 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- **New, 2018 detail on position:** "More than 65% of the viewing time above the fold was concentrated in the top half of the viewport." On general websites "more than 42% of the viewing time fell within the top 20% of the page." — [NN/g 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- **New, 2018 implications, quoted:** "Reserve the top of the page for high-priority content: key business and user goals … Keep major CTAs above the fold." Also: "Beware of false floors, which are increasingly common with modern minimalist designs … Include signifiers (such as cut-off text) to tell people that there is content below the fold." NN/g also notes: "page designs definitely tend to be too sparse now." — [NN/g 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- **New, fold magnitude:** "The Fold Manifesto: Why the Page Fold Still Matters," Amy Schade, February 1, 2015. "the 100 pixels just above the fold were viewed 102% more than the 100 pixels just below the fold." Google's ad-viewability data showed 73% vs 44% (a 66% drop). NN/g concludes: "84% is the average difference in how users treat info above vs. below the fold." Also: "Users do scroll, but only if what's above the fold is promising enough," and "Designs shouldn't need an arrow to tell users to scroll." — [NN/g Fold Manifesto](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/page-fold-manifesto/)
- **New, the fold today (2024):** "Homepage Design: 5 Fundamental Principles," Huei-Hsin Wang, March 15, 2024: "The concept of page fold remains crucial for homepages, despite the many available screen sizes and the ubiquity of responsive design … the content above the fold serves as a gatekeeper." The guidance is to position "the most critical content as high on the page as possible so it is visible above the fold regardless of users' device sizes," and to beware "false floors … especially with the growing popularity of image-based design." The article cites a full-bleed hero image with no cue below it as a failure (United States Steel). — [NN/g 2024](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/)
- **Inconsistency to note:** NN/g's own 2023 scrolljacking article still says "Web users spend 80% of their time looking at information above the page fold." That is the 2010 number, which NN/g's 2018 update superseded. — [NN/g Scrolljacking 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/)

### Inferences
- Cite "57% (first screen) / 74% (first two screens), NN/g 2018, desktop eyetracking." Do not use 80%, which is 2010 data on 1024×768 monitors. Do not describe either figure as mobile.
- For Loupe, NN/g's warnings about false floors and overly sparse pages both apply to a minimal, full-bleed image hero. The hero should visibly cut off the next section, for example a partial row of image cards. Relying on a scroll arrow is the pattern NN/g criticizes.

### Gaps
- NN/g has published no mobile-specific share-of-viewing-time figures comparable to the 57%/74% desktop numbers. I found none in the sitemap or in the articles I read.

## 3. NN/g on scrolljacking (2023), scroll-triggered animation and parallax

### Takeaway
**New. The first pass did not cover this.** NN/g's 2023 usability study found that scrolljacking "leads to more user pain than benefits." It is worst on mobile and worst when combined with text. If used at all, it should sit below the fold and carry little text. Related NN/g articles (2017, 2019, 2024) say text should never wait for a scroll-triggered animation. They also note that users mostly ignore parallax effects.

### Cited Findings
- **New:** "Scrolljacking 101," Sara Paul, August 6, 2023. Definition: "Scrolljacking (or scroll hijacking) is a design pattern that changes the speed and, sometimes, the direction of scrolling on a web page." Summary of findings: "websites with scrolljacking exhibit significant threats to user control and freedom, discoverability, attention, efficiency, and task success." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/)
- **New, findings quoted:** "The majority of our study participants were at least mildly disoriented by scrolljacking." "When [scrolljacks were excessively lengthy], users sometimes interpreted the scrolljack as a bug." "Task-oriented users did not have nearly as much patience for scrolljacking as users who were just exploring the site." "Pages that altered the rate and duration of scrolling while also requiring the user to read text exhibited the most severe usability issues." "Scrolljacking Is Worse on Mobile." Sticky navigation served as "an escape hatch." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/)
- **New, recommendations quoted:** "Our research findings tell us that scrolljacking leads to more user pain than benefits." The best practices are:
  - "The Scrolljack Should Progressively Disclose Valuable Information"
  - "Include Nonscrolljack Sections on the Page"
  - "Avoid Changing the Scroll Direction"
  - "Limit the Amount of Text In Scrolljacks"
  - "Avoid Scrolljacking on Mobile … in most cases, [the benefits] are not [worth the costs]"
  - "Use Scrolljacking Only Below the Fold … to highlight key calls to action already present above the fold."
  — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/)
- **New, scroll-triggered text:** "Scroll-Triggered Text Animations Delay Users," Aurora Harley, April 16, 2017. Guidelines: "Consider the context," "Use scroll-triggered animations for secondary content … rather than for the main bodies of text," and "Ensure that any animation triggered by scrolling only occurs once." NN/g also observes: "People don't necessarily distinguish … between actual system delays and delays due to visual effects." The article also notes that "subtle ones often go unnoticed" and that leisure and art-browsing visitors may enjoy them. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scroll-animations/)
- **New, parallax:** "What Parallax Lacks," Katie Sherwin, January 20, 2019. Problems listed: slow loading, unreadable fast-moving text, dizziness, effects missed entirely. "Parallax is just not that impressive to users. The people most likely to appreciate parallax effects? Other designers or developers … Today, users' reaction is the same as five years ago: indifference." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/parallax-usability/)
- **New, 2024 homepage rule:** "Don't use animation or motion for the sole purpose of drawing attention to an element on the homepage — moving elements are often assumed to be ads … parallax, autoforwarding carousels, and scrolljacking can cause disorientation and trigger vestibular disorders." "Avoid autoplaying videos." "Avoid animating crucial page elements like the logo, tagline, or main headline … Your tagline should be immediately visible on your homepage." The article cites Crown positively for scrolljack effects "while also offering a prominent option for users to disable the motion." — [NN/g 2024](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/)

### Inferences
- Loupe's audience (interior designers) is closer to NN/g's "browsing/art" context than to "task-focused B2B." Even so, the guidance converges: keep the hero headline, subhead and CTA static and visible at first paint. Use scroll-linked motion (for example GSAP ScrollTrigger) only on supporting imagery below the fold, play it once, never pin or hijack scrolling on mobile, and honor `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- NN/g's parallax finding that "designers appreciate it" is a double-edged point for a designer audience. They may notice the craft, but NN/g found no usability benefit. This is an inference; NN/g did not test designers as an audience.

### Gaps
- NN/g gives no sample size or participant count for the 2023 scrolljacking study on the article page.

## 4. NN/g on hero images: "photos as content" vs stock photos

### Takeaway
**Verified, with one wording correction.** NN/g's 2010 eyetracking article, last reviewed August 2026, says users scrutinize information-carrying images (products, real people) and ignore decorative "feel-good" images. NN/g's 2023–2024 imagery articles add three points: avoid stock photos, SaaS products should show "clear screenshots of their software," and too many similar images above the fold overwhelm users. A new August 2026 NN/g study found that AI-generated hero images were not penalized against stock photos when users were not told about AI.

### Cited Findings
- **Verified:** "Photos as Web Content," Jakob Nielsen, October 31, 2010 ("Last reviewed Aug. 13, 2026"). Summary: "Users pay close attention to photos and other images that contain relevant information but ignore fluffy pictures used to 'jazz up' web pages." The article describes two groups. "Some types of pictures are completely ignored. This is typically the case for big feel-good images that are purely decorative." "Photos of products and real people (as opposed to stock photos of models) often fall into this category [treated as important content and scrutinized]." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)
- **Verified, with figures added:** On Pottery Barn vs Amazon category pages, "Thumbnails of bookcases were studied intensely, whereas thumbnails of flat-panel TVs were mainly ignored." On Amazon, "only 18% of the viewing time was spent on the photos, while 82% was spent on the text." Other quotes: "jazzed-up = ignored," and "Invest in good photo shoots: a great photographer can add a fortune to your website's business value." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)
- **Corrected wording:** The first pass said "people who look like models drew less attention than ordinary-looking people." The NN/g article does not state that comparison in those words. It says real people and products are scrutinized "as opposed to stock photos of models," and that "users ignore stock photos of generic people." The claim may come from the book *Eyetracking Web Usability*, which I did not check. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)
- **New (2024):** "7 Tips for Memorable and Easy-to-Understand Imagery," Sara Paul, May 3, 2024. "#2: Avoid Stock Photos … users are likely to overlook photos they have seen previously … SaaS businesses can avoid stock photos by featuring clear screenshots of their software — which is what customers often want to see!" Also: "#3: Display Products in a Realistic Context," and "#5: Avoid Too Much Imagery: Too many visuals can be distracting and cause each of them to be less impactful … Hallmark's homepage features ten similar visuals above the fold … The eye is overwhelmed and unsure of where to focus." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/7-tips-memorable-imagery/)
- **New (2023):** "Using Imagery in Visual Design," Rachel Krause, July 23, 2023. "Prioritize Information-Carrying Images Over Decorative Images." "if your site features a free stock photo, many other sites may utilize that same free stock photo … they may also overlook other images on your site." On page weight: "The images on a webpage are too big if they don't fully appear as soon as you navigate to that page … aiming for a total page weight somewhere around 1–2MB." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/imagery-in-visual-design/)
- **New (2024 homepage):** "Ensure that featured imagery accurately reflects your brand … Avoid purely decorative or unhelpful graphics, as users tend to skip over them." A "generic stock image of water in its hero space" (Par Pacific) is the negative example. BP's facility image is the positive one. — [NN/g 2024](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/)
- **New (2014):** "Image-Focused Design: Is Bigger Better?", Kathryn Whitenton, September 28, 2014. Big hero images trade visibility of other elements for visceral appeal: "devoting this much emphasis to any single design element necessarily means that users are less likely to notice other elements." Her advice is to "Select images that have a strong relationship with brand goals … the purpose of your site is not just to showcase images (unless you're Flickr)." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/image-focused-design/)
- **New (2026):** "AI-Generated Images Can Perform as Well as Stock Photography," Rachel Banawa, August 21, 2026. Method: 77 U.S. adults rated six versions of a fictional consulting firm's page that differed only in the hero image (3 AI, 3 stock), each viewed for 10 s. Result: "When users didn't know whether an image was AI-generated, the images we tested did not create any perception penalty compared to stock photos." AI pages scored slightly higher. The difference was significant only for authenticity, at "only 0.4 points on a scale from 1 to 7." Caveats from the article: "When participants commented that they believed the imagery was AI-generated, they tended to also rate the site less favorably," and the results "may not generalize to other types of imagery, industries, or contexts." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-generated-images/)

### Inferences
- For Loupe, the strongest NN/g-backed hero is the real product UI showing real interior images. That is "information-carrying" imagery and the SaaS-screenshot case NN/g recommends. Generic people or lifestyle stock would be ignored.
- The Hallmark example (ten similar visuals above the fold) supports the first pass's warning against a dense mood-board wall in the hero. A few large, well-chosen images inside the UI frame fit the guidance better.
- The 2026 AI-image study compares AI images with stock photos. It does not compare them with real product imagery, so it gives no reason to replace real interiors with generated ones. Interior designers are likely to spot AI artifacts, which NN/g found lowers ratings. This last point is inference.

### Gaps
- NN/g's eyetracking work on images (2010) predates modern full-bleed hero layouts. I found no newer NN/g eyetracking specifically on hero images.

## 5. NN/g on carousels and auto-rotating content

### Takeaway
**Verified (the first pass noted NN/g carousel guidance could not be fetched; it is now confirmed).** NN/g recommends a static hero over a carousel, and recommends against auto-forwarding, especially on mobile. If a carousel is used, it should have 5 or fewer frames (desktop guidance), let users reach the last item in 3–4 swipes on mobile, and support swipe.

### Cited Findings
- **New:** "Designing Effective Carousels," Kara Pernice, September 14, 2013 ("Last reviewed Aug. 7, 2026"). Summary text: "on large or small viewports, people often scroll past carousels. A static hero or integrating content in the UI may be better solutions." "people frequently overlook them, together with all or some of the content within them." "a user often considers just the one image he sees." Guideline 1: "Include 5 or fewer frames within the carousel." On auto-forwarding: "Do not auto-forward on mobile devices, because: (1) it slows down the page, and (2) because pages are short, users often scroll quickly." Timing: "We use 3 words per second as a guideline." Ads: "Our eyetracking research reveals that animated ads get looked at 27% of the time." Conclusion: "Consider using a static carousel or hero image instead of a rotating one." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designing-effective-carousels/)
- **New:** "Auto-Forwarding Carousels and Accordions Annoy Users and Reduce Visibility," Jakob Nielsen, January 19, 2013. In a UK test, a user could not find a £100 offer shown in the largest type on the page because "it auto-rotates to show a new panel every 5 seconds." "Accordions and carousels should show a new panel only when users ask for it." Listed harms include accessibility for motor-impaired users, slow readers, "the discount deal is visible only 20% of the time," and "because it moves, users automatically assume that it might be an advertisement." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/auto-forwarding/)
- **New:** "Carousels on Mobile Devices," Raluca Budiu, August 19, 2018. "most people stop after viewing 3–4 different pages in the carousel … users should be able to reach the last item in the carousel in 3–4 steps." "The first item acts as a letter of recommendation for the rest of the content." "Make sure your carousel supports swipe." The article recommends a "page gutter" so carousel swipes are not confused with the iOS edge-swipe "Back" gesture. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-carousels/)
- **New (2024):** NN/g's homepage principles cite a full-screen carousel (Ovintive) as a negative example because "the absence of representative content made it challenging for new visitors to grasp the site's offerings." — [NN/g 2024](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/)

### Inferences
- For Loupe, any rotating "use-case" hero (save from Chrome, save from iPhone, describe to find) should be static tiles or a user-controlled sequence. A horizontally scrolling image strip below the hero is acceptable if it supports swipe, leaves edge gutters on mobile, and puts the most persuasive item first.

### Gaps
- None material. Runyon's click data on carousels (from the first pass) is not NN/g data and was not re-checked here.

## 6. NN/g on value propositions, homepage clarity and vague or "Introducing"-style hero copy

### Takeaway
**Verified and extended.** NN/g's 2024 homepage principles say the hero should state "who you are and what you do" with an explicit tagline, avoid "cheerful 'welcomes,'" and avoid jargon and feature-driven language. A July 2025 NN/g article warns that "Powered by AI" is not a value proposition and that the word "AI" can lower conversion. NN/g has no article specifically about "Introducing X" headlines. The nearest guidance is on content-free taglines and generic welcomes.

### Cited Findings
- **New (2024):** "Homepage Design: 5 Fundamental Principles," Huei-Hsin Wang, March 15, 2024. "Treat your homepage as an elevator pitch to prospective customers, quickly and clearly conveying what your organization does and what users can accomplish on your site. Don't make people guess." Guideline 2.2: "Include a tagline that explicitly conveys what your site or company does … your tagline should be simple, scannable, and to the point." Also: "cheerful 'welcomes' on homepages aren't helpful as they do not provide any information about your company. Transform these greetings into meaningful taglines instead." A "generic welcome message in the hero space" (Qurate) was criticized for "pushing the company brief below the fold." — [NN/g 2024](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/)
- **New (2024):** Guideline 2.3: "Your homepage should communicate your unique value proposition clearly, usually through a descriptive tagline and compelling content in the hero space … 'Why should I choose this site/company over others?'" Also: "Use words that resonate with people and avoid jargon, business terminology, and feature-driven language." Guideline 3.2: "Provide specific examples of your site's content." Guideline 4.1: "Generic language like Click Here, Explore, or Learn More does not tell users what they will get." — [NN/g 2024](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/)
- **New (2025):** "'Powered By AI' Is Not a Value Proposition," Pavel Samsonov, July 4, 2025. "Good Value Propositions Are About the What, Not the How." "relative descriptors such as 'easier' or 'more powerful' are often a sign that the value proposition is not defined." Examples of poorly defined value propositions: "Redesigned UI that's easier to use / Ten exciting new features / More powerful than ever before." Well-defined examples include "Never miss a bill payment again with automatic payments." Also: "AI is a technology, not a user benefit. In a crowded market, it is not an effective differentiator." The article says the presence of the term "AI" in product descriptions "can trigger fear and reduce the likelihood of conversion," citing Cicek, Gursoy & Lu (2024), *Journal of Hospitality Marketing & Management*. Grammarly is the positive example: its landing page "addresses users' potential anxieties about AI by emphasizing goals like find the words you need." — [NN/g 2025](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/powered-by-ai-is-not-a-value-proposition/)
- **New, content-free taglines:** "Tagline Blues: What's the Site About?", Jakob Nielsen, July 21, 2001. "A website's tagline must explain what the company does and what makes it unique among competitors. Two questions can help you assess your own tagline: Would it work just as well for competitors? Would any company ever claim the opposite?" He calls vague taglines "basically content-free word count." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tagline-blues-whats-the-site-about/)
- **New, vague link copy:** NN/g's 2013 Siemens example criticized the slogan "Rewarding.Life.Style" as "content-free content to at least 99% of humans outside Siemens' marketing department." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/auto-forwarding/)
- **New, CTAs (2017):** "First Impressions Matter," Therese Fessenden, October 1, 2017. "Avoid competing, equally salient calls-to-action. If you must have two CTAs, style them so that their priority is obvious." "Too many colors (more than 4) and too many fonts (more than 2) on a page … can result in the 'rainbow effect.'" The article recommends measuring first impressions with "Screen-timeout testing." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-impressions-human-automaticity/)
- **Could not verify:** I found no NN/g article that specifically addresses "Introducing [Product]"-style hero headlines. Searches of NN/g's sitemap for "introducing," "hero," "headline" and "vague" turned up only the articles cited above.

### Inferences
- A Loupe hero such as "Introducing Loupe" or "AI-powered visual library" fails NN/g's tests. It names no user outcome. "AI-powered" could describe any competitor (the Tagline Blues test), and the 2025 article says "AI" alone may add anxiety. The hero should state the outcome in designer language, for example finding saved images by describing them. AI tagging is better presented as the means, shown in the product visual.
- The Tagline Blues "opposite" test is a cheap copy check for the redesign. "Beautifully organized" fails it. "Describe the image you saved and Loupe finds it" passes. These examples are illustrative, not tested.

### Gaps
- No NN/g quantitative study isolates value-proposition clarity in a hero against bounce. NN/g's evidence here is qualitative usability testing plus the 2011 dwell-time interpretation.
- I did not open the Cicek, Gursoy & Lu (2024) paper NN/g cites. Its "AI lowers purchase intention" finding comes from hospitality and service products and may not transfer to design tools.

## 7. Google Research: Tuch et al. 2012, "The role of visual complexity and prototypicality regarding first impression of websites"

### Takeaway
**Verified, with corrections to the timing conditions and with sample sizes added.** There was no 17/50/500 ms design. **Study 1** used 50 vs 500 vs 1000 ms (between subjects, **n = 59**, 119 analyzed screenshots). **Study 2** used 17 vs 33 vs 50 ms (**n = 82**, 80 screenshots). Participants rated **perceived beauty** of masked screenshots of unfamiliar **company homepages**. Low visual complexity plus high prototypicality scored best. Complexity effects appeared even at 17 ms, and prototypicality effects grew with exposure time. Stay/leave was not measured. The Google blog's line about deciding "whether they'll stay or leave" is framing, not a result.

### Cited Findings
- **Verified, citation:** Tuch, A. N., Presslaber, E. E., Stöcklin, M., Opwis, K., & Bargas-Avila, J. A. (2012). *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies*, 70(11), 794–811. DOI 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2012.06.003. Affiliations: University of Basel and Google/YouTube UX Research, Zurich. — [Google Research publication page](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/); [Univ. Basel edoc record](https://edoc.unibas.ch/21676/)
- **Verified, abstract (exact):** "In the first study, 119 screenshots of real websites varying in VC (low vs. medium vs. high) and PT (low vs. high) were rated on perceived aesthetics. Screenshot presentation time was varied as a between-subject factor (50 ms vs. 500 ms vs. 1000 ms). Results reveal that VC and PT affect participants' aesthetics ratings within the first 50 ms of exposure. In the second study presentation times were shortened to 17, 33 and 50ms. Results suggest that VC and PT affect aesthetic perception even within 17ms, though the effect of PT is less pronounced than the one of VC. With increasing presentation time the effect of PT becomes as influential as the VC effect … Overall, websites with low VC and high PT were perceived as highly appealing." — [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/)
- **Corrected, timing conditions:** The conditions were 50/500/1000 ms (Study 1) and 17/33/50 ms (Study 2). There was no 500 ms condition in the 17 ms study. Measured mean exposures in Study 2 were 16.67, 33.27 and 49.98 ms. — [Tuch et al. manuscript, Google-hosted PDF](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/38315.pdf)
- **New, sample sizes (closes a first-pass gap):**
  - **Study 1:** "n = 59 participants (45 females), mainly undergraduate psychology students of the University of Basel … mean age of 25.4 years," randomly assigned to the three exposure groups. 120 screenshots were shown and 119 analyzed, because one was lost to a logging error.
  - **Study 2:** "A sample of 82 participants (57 females) … mean age of 27.3 years … They had no education in either visual design or web design." Group sizes were 17 ms n = 30, 33 ms n = 24, 50 ms n = 28. Only 80 pages were shown, because the medium-complexity level was dropped.
  — [Tuch et al. manuscript PDF](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/38315.pdf)
- **New, what was measured:** "Perceived beauty was assessed by means of a visual analog scale (VAS) with the anchors ugly and beautiful," rescaled 1–9. Participants were "asked to rate the web pages intuitively without thinking too much." Each screenshot was followed by a random-noise mask, because perception persists "for about 250 ms after the stimulus is extinguished." The study ran in a university computer lab at 1000×800 px. Stimuli were company homepages only, pre-rated for complexity and prototypicality by 267 online raters and screened as unfamiliar to Swiss participants. — [Tuch et al. manuscript PDF](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/38315.pdf)
- **New, main results detail:**
  - **Study 1:** Significant VC × PT interaction (ηp² = .604). Main effects of VC (ηp² = .581) and PT (ηp² = .812). No main effect of presentation time. The prototypicality effect was "much more pronounced for the low and medium (cohen's d = 1.96, respectively 1.79) than for the high complexity level (d = .24)."
  - **Study 2:** At 17 ms, the effect was "more pronounced for VC than for PT (ηp² = .38, respectively ηp² = .19), whereas at 33 ms and 50 ms the effects have approximately the same magnitude."
  — [Tuch et al. manuscript PDF](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/38315.pdf)
- **New, conclusions (exact):** "Users prefer websites with low visual complexity and high prototypicality. Websites of low prototypicality are generaly [sic] judged as being unattractive – in fact, this counts for websites of high as well as low complexity. Both factors already influence the aesthetic judgments after a very short presentation time of 17 ms." — [Tuch et al. manuscript PDF](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/38315.pdf)
- **New, the authors' own limitations:** "(1) Passive viewing … the findings cannot be associated directly with how websites are perceived in real life"; "(2) Demographic factors … our sample consisted of a rather young population"; "(3) Website types. The presented study was conducted with company websites"; "(4) … nor can we derive conclusions about which factors lead to high or low complexity/prototypicality." — [Tuch et al. manuscript PDF](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/38315.pdf)
- **Verified, with framing caveat:** The Google Research blog post "Users love simple and familiar designs – Why websites need to make a great first impression" is dated August 29, 2012 and was written by Javier Bargas-Avila. It says: "In less than 50 milliseconds, users build an initial 'gut feeling' that helps them decide whether they'll stay or leave." It also says: "if the visual complexity of a website is high, users perceive it as less beautiful, even if the design is familiar. And if the design is unfamiliar -- i.e., the site has low prototypicality -- users judge it as uglier, even if it's simple." The study itself measured beauty ratings only. The "stay or leave" link is the blog's interpretation. — [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs-why-websites-need-to-make-a-great-first-impression/)
- **Note on the URL:** The first pass cited ai.googleblog.com. The post now lives at research.google/blog (link above).

### Inferences
- For Loupe, the transferable result is that a simple, conventional-looking product landing page will win the beauty snap judgment. An unusual layout risks looking "ugly" even if it is simple. Note the scope: the participants were lay young adults explicitly without design training, and the stimuli were corporate homepages. Interior designers may judge differently. That is untested.
- The VC × PT interaction means simplicity pays off most when the layout is also prototypical. A minimal but unconventional hero would not get the full benefit.

### Gaps
- My source is Google's hosted author manuscript (38315.pdf). Page numbers and minor wording may differ from the published IJHCS version, which I did not access through ScienceDirect.

## 8. Google 2016 and Google/SOASTA 2017 mobile speed figures (53% after 3 s; bounce probability +32% from 1 s to 3 s)

### Takeaway
**Verified, with source corrections.**
- **The 53% figure** comes from **Google Analytics benchmark data (n = 3.7K mobile sites, March 2016)**. It does not come from the "10,000+ domains" load-time analysis or from SOASTA. Its original wording is "53% of visits are likely to be abandoned if pages take longer than 3 seconds to load."

It appears in a chart in Daniel An's Think with Google article (February 2017, updated February 20, 2018). It is a relative increase in modeled bounce probability, not 32 percentage points.

## 9. NN/g guidance (2022–2026) on mobile homepages and first screens

### Takeaway
**New.** I found no NN/g article dedicated to mobile homepages or first screens dated 2022–2026. The relevant recent guidance is spread across four sources: the State of Mobile UX (January 2023), Homepage Design principles (March 2024, which apply the fold "regardless of users' device sizes"), Scrolljacking 101 (August 2023, "Avoid Scrolljacking on Mobile"), and older still-cited mobile articles (carousels 2018, auto-forwarding). The common message is to put content before decoration on the small screen, because mobile users scroll only when the top looks promising. Overlays and motion also cause problems.

### Cited Findings
- **New (2023):** "The State of Mobile User Experience," Raluca Budiu, January 1, 2023. The research base for the 5th edition of NN/g's mobile report was "19 US-based participants who tested 60 sites and applications." On small screens: "gone are the days when most of the homepage was used for UI elements and the content was pushed below the fold. However, the small-screen lesson is not completely learned — we still see pages needlessly lengthened by images and graphics that are decorative and unnecessary, sometimes under the assumption that 'mobile users know to scroll.' While that is true, it is not true that mobile users will scroll for the sake of scrolling. They scroll only if they think that the page content is promising enough." — [NN/g 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/state-mobile-ux/)
- **New (2023):** On overlays: "A lot of sites and apps use several layers of overlays … overlays tend to cause many usability problems." On in-app browsers: they "cause disorientation and confusion … the app UI can interfere with the UI of the page that is displayed in the browser." — [NN/g 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/state-mobile-ux/)
- **New (2024):** "Positioning the most critical content as high on the page as possible so it is visible above the fold regardless of users' device sizes." "Avoid popup windows and splash screens unless legally required … offer value to your visitors before requesting anything from them." Speed: "Content should be readily accessible to users upon their arrival." The article cites a homepage where "During the first 6.4 seconds, no visible content was presented." — [NN/g 2024](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/homepage-design-principles/)
- **New (2023):** "Scrolljacking Is Worse on Mobile … When sites did not carry scrolljacking over from desktop to mobile, users had a much better experience." Also: "many sticky navigation menus were collapsed on mobile and thus harder to find." — [NN/g 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/)
- **New (older mobile guidance still cited):** "Do not auto-forward on mobile devices" (2013, reviewed 2026). — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designing-effective-carousels/) "Writing for mobile readers requires even harsher editing than writing for the Web. Mobile use implies less patience for filler copy." (Jakob Nielsen, "Mobile Content: If in Doubt, Leave It Out," October 9, 2011.) — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/condense-mobile-content/)

### Inferences
- For Loupe's mobile first screen: put the headline, a one-line outcome and the primary CTA at first paint, with a glimpse of real product imagery that also signals there is more below. Avoid decorative spacer images, pinned scroll sections, cookie or signup overlays before value is shown, and auto-advancing elements.
- Many designers may arrive from Instagram or Pinterest in-app browsers. NN/g's 2023 note about conflicting UI in in-app browsers argues for checking that sticky bottom CTAs are not hidden by in-app browser toolbars. This is inference, extended from NN/g's ESPN example.

### Gaps
- No NN/g study dated 2022–2026 reports mobile first-screen attention figures, mobile homepage conversion data, or specific guidance on landing-page heroes for mobile. The full Mobile UX report (5th edition) is paywalled and was not read.
- NN/g's 2018 57%/74% figures are desktop data. Using them to describe mobile behavior is an extrapolation.
